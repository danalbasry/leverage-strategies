Question 1 of 7

What is an AI agent, in plain terms?

An AI agent is an AI tool you give a goal instead of a single question. It works out the steps, uses tools such as a web browser, your email or your files to carry them out, and comes back when it's done, stuck, or about to do something that needs your OK.

The companies that build them say the same thing in their own words. OpenAI's guide for builders says: "Agents are systems that independently accomplish tasks on your behalf." It adds that simple chatbots that don't use the AI to control the workflow are not agents (OpenAI). Anthropic, which makes Claude, says that in an agent the AI decides its own steps and tools (Anthropic).

Here's the difference for a made-up café, Laneway Coffee in Toronto:

What it does Made-up Laneway Coffee example
ChatbotAnswers one question at a time; you do the work"Write a reply to this catering email"
Fixed automationFollows steps someone set in advance, the same way every time"When an email arrives with 'catering' in the subject, add the label Catering"
AgentDecides the steps itself to reach a goal, using your tools"Go through this week's inbox, sort it, and draft replies to the routine ones for me to check"

Anthropic advises "the simplest solution possible", because agents mean "higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors" (Anthropic). If one prompt or a fixed rule works, use that.

Question 3 of 7

What are agents good at now, and what do they still get wrong?

Agents are good at short jobs with a clear finish line that you can check in minutes. They still struggle with long jobs, with knowing when to stop, and with telling your instructions apart from instructions hidden in what they read.

The independent tests agree:

  • Short web jobs: about 6 in 10. On 300 tasks across 136 live websites, checked by people, OpenAI's Operator agent completed 61.3%, Claude Computer Use 3.7 completed 56.3%, and a tool called Browser Use 30.0%. The researchers say earlier, higher scores showed "over-optimism" (Online-Mind2Web, arXiv). That test is from 2025, and the tools have changed since.
  • Long jobs: about 1 in 5. On 108 real computer workflows that take a skilled person a median of about 1.6 hours, the best agent finished 20.6% from start to end (OSWorld 2.0, arXiv).

Two real failures show why the approval step matters:

  • It bought eggs nobody asked it to buy. A Washington Post columnist asked OpenAI's Operator to "find the cheapest set of a dozen eggs I can have delivered". He says he never asked it to buy anything. It bought them on Instacart, with tip and fees, for US$31.43, and reported the total as US$19.68 (AI Incident Database, from the Post's February 7, 2025 column).
  • An email told it what to do. Security researchers at Radware showed that one email with hidden instructions could make ChatGPT's research agent, connected to Gmail, send private details such as names and addresses to an outside web address when someone later asked it to summarize their inbox. "No need to click or open a malicious link." OpenAI fixed it (Radware, September 18, 2025).

That second kind of attack is called prompt injection: instructions planted in something the agent reads. Anthropic's own safety page for Claude Cowork says "Web content is a primary vector for prompt injection attacks" (Claude Help Center). It's the main reason this page's first job never lets the agent send anything.

What's a good first job to hand one?

A good first job reads and drafts, never sends or pays, touches as little customer information as possible, and gives you something you can check in a few minutes. Which job that is depends on what eats your week and how sensitive your information is.

How do I hand it over, step by step?

Write the job down as a short brief, run it on a practice inbox of made-up emails first, check the results against an answer key, and only then point it at one label in your real inbox, drafts only. Give yourself an hour for the first run.

Make a new, free email account that holds nothing real, and send it these six made-up emails from your own address. The brief: fill in the brackets

You're helping me with the inbox for [BUSINESS], a [TYPE OF BUSINESS] in [CITY]. Go through the emails in [THIS INBOX OR LABEL] from the last 7 days. Sort each one into: Reply today, Needs me, Bills and records, Ignore, or Suspicious. For each Reply today email, write a draft reply using ONLY the facts below. Step 3: Run it in your tool

In ChatGPT

  1. Connect Gmail to ChatGPT from Settings, signing in to your practice account, and read the access it asks for before you agree.
  2. In a new chat, turn on agent mode from the tools menu and paste the brief. Full agent use needs a paid plan; Free gets limited use in the desktop app only (ChatGPT).
  3. Watch it work. If it reaches anything that would send, stop it.
  4. Once the first run is right, you can make it weekly: OpenAI says you can "schedule completed tasks to recur automatically" (OpenAI). Scheduled tasks need Go or a higher plan (ChatGPT).

In Claude

  1. Connect Gmail with your practice account (Google Workspace connectors are "available for all users", Claude Help Center).
  2. Set the Gmail tools that send, reply and forward to "Always ask" under Customize, Connectors, Permissions. Claude then asks before each use "in every mode" (Claude Help Center).
  3. Use a normal chat or Cowork in Manual mode, and paste the brief. Don't use Research mode for this: "During research, Claude can use tools from your connectors automatically, without asking for approval each time" (Claude Help Center).
  4. To make it weekly, type /schedule in a Cowork task (paid plans). With send tools on "Always ask", Anthropic says a scheduled task "may wait for your answer or finish without the tool". That's what you want here: the drafts get written, nothing gets sent.

In Gemini

  1. Open Gmail with an eligible Google Workspace or Google AI plan and open Gemini in Gmail (Google).
  2. Paste the brief. Gemini summarizes, sorts and drafts. It won't delete, archive or label anything until you confirm, and you get 60 seconds to undo.

Your new practice account won't have Gemini. Open Gemini in your usual account, start a new chat, and paste the six emails, then the brief.

In Copilot

  1. In Outlook, open Copilot and ask it to summarize each thread and draft replies, using the brief.
  2. For each reply, have Copilot draft it, then review it, select Keep it, edit, and select Send yourself (Microsoft).

Your new practice account won't have Copilot. Open Copilot in your usual account, start a new chat, and paste the six emails, then the brief.

In Muse

Muse can send email and keeps an audit trail of what it did (Meta), but I couldn't confirm on Meta's pages that it connects to a business Gmail or Outlook inbox. Where it fits well today is the Instagram and Facebook side of the same job: drafting replies and posts for you to approve.

Step 5: Point it at one label, for one week

Before you connect your real inbox, turn model training off (ChatGPT: Data controls; Claude: Privacy settings), and use a business plan where you can. Customer emails don't belong in a personal account. [DAN: CONFIRM MENU NAMES AND EACH VENDOR'S BUSINESS-PLAN TRAINING PAGE.]

When the practice run is clean, connect your real inbox, but give the agent one label to work on (for example, Catering), not the whole mailbox. The label limits what you ask for, not what the tool can see. Only a Zapier label trigger truly limits it. Keep it to drafts for at least a week. Each morning, open your Drafts folder, read each draft, fix it, and send it yourself. Write down roughly how long the label took before and after, so you know whether it's worth keeping.

Question 6 of 7

Where must a person approve, and how do I set that up?

A person approves anything that leaves your business or can't easily be undone: sending a message, paying or ordering, posting, deleting, and changing a record. Each tool has a different default (see the comparison table above), so set it yourself before the first run.

Three habits matter more than any setting:

  • You stay responsible. Anthropic's terms are typical: "You remain responsible for all actions taken by Claude performed on your behalf", including messages sent, purchases and actions taken by scheduled tasks (Claude Help Center).

Question 7 of 7

What are the risks and rules?

The big risks are an agent following instructions hidden in an email, an agent telling a customer something that isn't true, and customer information going where it shouldn't. The rules haven't changed because a tool did the work: what an agent sends in your name is yours.

  • Hidden instructions. Treat every email, web page and attachment the agent reads as information, not orders. That's why the brief says so, why email 6 is in the practice inbox, and why the first job never sends.
  • Wrong answers in your name. In Canada, a tribunal held Air Canada liable for negligent misrepresentation after its website chatbot told a customer he could claim a bereavement fare after booking, which the airline's own policy didn't allow (Cassels, on Moffatt v. Air Canada, February 14, 2024). If your agent drafts a price, a policy or a date, check it against your fact sheet before it goes out.
  • Customer information. Give the agent the smallest slice of your inbox that does the job, and keep health, card and bank details out of it.

In the US

  • If an agent ever calls or texts customers with an AI voice, get their prior express consent first: the FCC treats AI voices as "artificial or prerecorded voices" under the TCPA (Troutman Pepper Locke).

In Canada

  • Privacy regulators say generative AI does "not occupy a space outside of current legislative frameworks", and advise using "anonymized or de-identified information within prompts" where possible (Office of the Privacy Commissioner, December 7, 2023). PIPEDA applies to your customer emails whoever reads them, and Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec have their own private-sector laws (OPC).
  • CASL covers messages meant to sell: they need consent, identification and an unsubscribe link, and penalties reach CA$10 million per violation (CRTC). Keep drafts to what the customer asked. [DAN: FOR A QUEBEC READER, ADD A NOTE ON QUEBEC'S PRIVACY LAW BEFORE THEY CONNECT A CUSTOMER INBOX.]
