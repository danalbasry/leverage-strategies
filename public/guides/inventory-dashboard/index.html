Question 2 of 8

What can my sales export tell me about my stock?

It tells you how fast each item sells, which is half of every reorder decision. The other half, how much you have and how long a delivery takes, comes from a stock count and your supplier, not the sales file.

Every till in the table exports item sales to a spreadsheet file. The columns you need are an item name, a code that matches the stock count (usually the SKU), and the quantity sold. Shopify's "POS total sales by product variant" report gives Product title, Variant title, Variant SKU and Net quantity, where net quantity "equates to number of sold items - number of returned items" (Shopify). Use net, not gross, so returns don't count as sales.

In the US

Square's Custom Item report (Reports, then Custom) exports to CSV or Excel with item name, quantity sold, gross sales and net sales (Square). Square's help page doesn't list a SKU column for that report, so check your file. If there's no SKU, match on the item name and keep names identical in both files.

In Canada

The same exports work the same way. On Square in Canada, the stock count export is included on the free plan (Square Canada), which gives you the second file for nothing.

Three traps I check for in every export:

  • Rows with no SKU. Custom sales (gift wrap, a one-off charge) often have none. They can't be matched to stock, so leave them out and say so.
  • Items that didn't sell are missing. Toast's Product Mix report hides items with no sales by default (Toast). An item that's missing from the sales file is often the one you most need to see, so the stock count is the master list, not the sales file.
  • Two reports, two totals. Toast says its Product Mix report "calculates net sales differently from reports like the Sales Summary" (it leaves out service charges and tips, for example) (Toast). Compare a dashboard total with the same report it came from.

  • Sold and a week. Net units sold in the export, and that number divided by the days covered, times 7.

  • Check before you order. How many rows it read, returns taken off, rows left out and why, and any SKU in the sales file that's missing from the stock count.

How do I work out what to reorder?

Order when the stock you have, plus what's already coming, would run out before a new delivery arrives, with a buffer for a busy week or a late truck. That's the reorder point, and it's standard textbook practice.

Reorder point. Expected sales during the supplier's lead time, plus safety stock. An open operations textbook writes it as ROP = d̄L + SS: average demand per period times lead time, plus safety stock (ISBM Library). Moneris, a Canadian payments company, gives a shop-sized example: "A candle that sells 10 units a week with a two-week lead time and five units of buffer gets a reorder point of 25" (Moneris).

The made-up cactus pot, worked through:

  1. Sold 132 in 28 days, so about 4.71 a day.
  2. The supplier takes 7 days. Expected sales while you wait: 4.71 × 7 = about 33.
  3. Safety stock of 7 days' sales: about 33 more.
  4. Reorder point: 66. With 18 on hand and none on order, it's well past time.

Compare against what you have plus what's coming. The same textbook calls this inventory position: on hand, plus on order, minus anything already promised to customers (ISBM Library).

Safety stock, two ways. Start with a number of days of sales, which is what the dashboard does (7 by default; change it). When you have a few months of daily sales, the textbook method sizes the buffer from how much sales swing: SS = z × σd × √L, where σd is the standard deviation of daily sales, L is lead time in days, and z sets how often you're willing to run out. Typical values are 1.28 for 90%, 1.645 for 95%, 2.05 for 98% and 2.326 for 99% (ISBM Library). No source I opened names one right level for a small shop. That's a business call: how bad is it if this item runs out?

Weeks of cover. On hand divided by average units sold a week. Toolio, which sells retail planning software, gives the example of 800 on hand and 100 a week: 8 weeks (Toolio). It's the quickest way to read the dashboard: if cover is shorter than the lead time plus your buffer, check what's already on order before you order more.

Sell-through. The share of what you had that sold. Shopify defines it as quantity sold divided by quantity sold plus quantity still in inventory, over the most recent 30 days (Shopify). The dashboard uses the same formula over the days in your export. Other sources divide by units received or by stock at the start of the period (Toolio), and those give different numbers, so compare items using one definition only. Low sell-through with lots of cover is the signal not to reorder, however popular the item feels.

How much to order. A reorder point says when, not how much. The dashboard suggests enough for lead time, safety stock and the days until your next order (14 by default). Then round to case sizes and minimums, and check the cash.

Order when your stock, plus what's coming, would run out before the next delivery. Keep a little extra for a busy week or a late truck. That level is called the reorder point.

Moneris, a Canadian payments company, gives an example: a candle that sells 10 a week, with two weeks to deliver and 5 extra, has a reorder point of 25 (Moneris).

The made-up cactus pot, step by step:

  1. It sold 132 in 28 days. That's about 4.7 a day.
  2. Delivery takes 7 days. You'll sell about 33 while you wait.
  3. Keep 7 days of extra stock: about 33 more.
  4. Reorder point: 66. There are only 18, so order now.

How do I build my own in ChatGPT or Claude?

Upload your two files, ask for the same sums with code, and ask for the result as a page or a spreadsheet. Every tool here can do it; the right one is the one your business already lives in.

Clean the files first. OpenAI's own advice is to "Include descriptive column headers in the first row" and "Use one row per record" (OpenAI). Delete any customer columns before you upload (see the privacy section).

The prompt I'd start with (words in brackets are yours to fill in):

Prompt

I've attached two files from my [type of shop]. File 1 is a sales export covering [number] days: one row per item sold, with a SKU and a net quantity. File 2 is my stock count: SKU, on hand, on order and supplier lead time in days. Use code for every calculation, not estimates. First, tell me how many rows you read, how many you left out and why, and any SKU that's in one file but not the other. Then for each item give: units sold, units a week, weeks of cover (on hand divided by units a week), reorder point (average daily sales × (lead time in days + [7] safety days)), and a status: "Reorder now" if on hand plus on order is at or below the reorder point. Sort by status. Show the formulas you used. Tell me anything the files can't answer.

In ChatGPT

  1. Attach both files in a chat and paste the prompt. Up to 10 files can go in one conversation (OpenAI).
  2. Check the work: click the blue [>_] link at the end of a message to see the code it ran (OpenAI).
  3. Ask for the result as a downloadable spreadsheet with formulas, so next week you can paste new numbers in. The code environment can't fetch anything from the web (OpenAI), so everything it needs has to be in your files.

In Claude

  1. Turn on Settings, then Capabilities, then "Code execution and file creation" (called "Create and edit files" in the newer layout). It's on every plan, including Free, and the limit is 30 MB per file in and out (Claude Help Center).
  2. Attach both files and paste the prompt.
  3. Ask: "Now make this an interactive dashboard I can paste next week's files into." Claude builds it as an artifact. Anthropic's list of what an artifact can be includes "a dashboard, or a small interactive tool". Artifacts start private to you, and you can make them on the Free plan (Claude Help Center). Saving data inside an artifact needs Pro or higher.

In Gemini

If your stock list lives in Google Sheets, keep it there. Open the sheet, click Ask Gemini at the top right, and ask for the same columns as formulas. Gemini in Sheets can build tables, formulas, charts, pivot tables and conditional formatting. Two catches from Google's own page: a chart it makes "doesn't link or respond to changes in the original data set", and you lose the chat history when you reload, so insert what you want into the sheet before you leave (Google). On Google Workspace it needs Business Standard or higher; Business Starter doesn't include it (Google Workspace).

In Copilot

If you run on Excel, select the Copilot button in Excel and ask for formula columns: weeks of cover, reorder point and a status column, plus an XLOOKUP that pulls on-hand from the stock sheet. Microsoft lists formula columns, lookups, charts and PivotTables among what it can make, and says to "review, edit, and verify anything Copilot creates for you" (Microsoft).

Ask for formulas, not typed totals. A spreadsheet with formulas can be checked and reused next week. A pasted number can't.

I left Meta's Muse agent out of the build steps. I use it for research, but I couldn't confirm on Meta's own pages that it analyses spreadsheet files.

Question 7 of 8

Can I trust the numbers?

Not without checking, because AI tools still miscount on exactly this kind of file. In a November 2026 benchmark of 70 realistic workplace spreadsheet requests, with strict pass-or-fail grading, the best model passed only 38.3% of its attempts (GDP.xlsx, arXiv). It's a preprint, and the authors' company, Surge AI, sells evaluation services.

The mistakes look small and sound confident. In a vendor-run test by OWOX, which sells an analytics product, Claude was asked how many units a demo shop sold in a week. All 3 runs answered 305; the shop counts completed orders only, which gives 292. The model had counted 13 returned units as sold, and said so in its answer (OWOX). In 36 runs it never invented a column. The error was the definition, not made-up data.

Five checks before you order anything:

  1. Re-add one total yourself. In the sample: 1,014 units and $12,776.00 from items with a SKU.
  2. Count the rows. Ask how many rows it read and left out. The sample has 276, with 3 left out for having no SKU.
  3. Say your definitions first. Net quantity (after returns), which days, which locations.
  4. Look for the items that aren't there. Zero-sales items and SKUs missing from one file are where stock hides.
  5. Ask for formulas or code, and open them. In ChatGPT that's the [>_] link; in Claude, ask it to show the code.

Question 8 of 8

What should I keep private?

Keep customer details out of the file before it goes anywhere. An inventory dashboard needs item, SKU, date, quantity and sales. It doesn't need names, emails, phone numbers, addresses or order notes, so delete those columns first. That's my rule, not a law, but it means a leak of the working file exposes your stock, not your customers.

Use a business plan where you can, and on a personal plan switch model training off first. In ChatGPT, that's "Model improvement" under Data controls, and a Temporary Chat won't help improve OpenAI's models (OpenAI). In Claude, it's "Model Improvement" in your Privacy Settings, and Incognito chats aren't used to improve Claude even with it on (Anthropic). On business plans, ChatGPT Business doesn't train on business data by default (OpenAI), and Claude's file feature carries its own warning: hidden instructions in a file or web page could trick Claude into "sending information from its context" elsewhere, so only upload files you made yourself (Claude Help Center).

In the US

The IRS's general rule for keeping business records is "three years depending on the action, expense and event recorded in the document" (IRS). Your state's sales tax rules may ask for longer, so check them. Keep the original exports in your till or accounting system; the dashboard copy doesn't need to be the record.

In Canada

Keep business records for six years from the end of the last tax year they relate to, at your place of business or home in Canada unless the CRA gives written permission otherwise (CRA). Under PIPEDA, personal information can only be used for the purposes it was collected for unless the person consents or the law requires it, and collection is limited to what's needed (Office of the Privacy Commissioner). Uploading a customer list to build a stock dashboard isn't what it was collected for, which is one more reason to strip those columns first.
