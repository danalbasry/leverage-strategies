Question 1 of 7

How often does AI get things wrong, really?

It depends almost entirely on the job: from about 2 in 100 summaries of a document you hand it, to more than half of answers about where a quote came from. There is no single error rate, and anyone who quotes one without naming the task is selling something.

It depends on the job. Sometimes it is about 2 in 100. Sometimes it is more than half.

It depends on the job. When it sums up a page you give it, the best AI adds something false about 2 times in 100 (Vectara). When it has to find where a quote came from, it is wrong more than half the time (Tow Center).

  • Summarising a document you supply. On Vectara's leaderboard the best model added something that was not in the document in 1.8% of summaries. Well-known models sat higher: GPT-5.4 at 7.0%, Claude Opus 5.5 at 9.9%, Gemini 3.1 Pro preview at 10.4%. The worst was 24.2% (Vectara, updated 24 Nov 2026). Vectara sells hallucination-detection tools and grades with its own model, so this is a vendor-run test.
  • Answering news questions. In a study by 22 public broadcasters in 18 countries, including CBC/Radio-Canada, 45% of answers from ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Perplexity had at least one significant issue. 31% had sourcing problems and 20% had major accuracy problems, "including hallucinated details and outdated information" (EBU, 22 Oct 2025).
  • Finding the source. Eight AI search tools gave wrong answers to more than 60% of 1,600 requests to name the article behind a quote. ChatGPT Search got 134 of 200 wrong, signalled doubt only 15 times and never declined to answer (Tow Center, Columbia Journalism Review, 6 Mar 2025).
  • Repeating false claims. Ten leading chatbots repeated false news claims 35% of the time in August 2025, up from 18% a year earlier, and answered every prompt instead of declining (NewsGuard, 4 Sep 2025).

The pattern that matters for a business is not the percentage. It is that the AI rarely says "I don't know", so a wrong answer looks exactly as tidy and confident as a right one.

So the useful question is not "how accurate is AI?" but "what am I using it for, and what happens if it's wrong?" How much checking a job needs depends on what a mistake would cost, how easy it is to undo, and how much you rely on the AI's words as they are.

The big problem: AI almost never says "I don't know." A wrong answer looks just as neat as a right one.

What quick checks catch most mistakes?

The five checks that work all use something outside the AI: the source itself, a calculator, your own document, a different AI, and the official rule for your place. Asking the same AI "are you sure?" mostly doesn't help.

Use checks that go outside the AI. Asking the same AI "are you sure?" does not work well.

Five checks. Each one uses something outside the AI.

  1. Open the source.

    Click every link behind a number, price, date, rule or quote. Check that the page opens, that it is the original and not a copy, and that it actually says what the AI claims. For a quote, search the page for four or five distinctive words.

  2. Do the math outside the chat.yourself.

    Paste your price list, menu or policy and say: "Answer only from this. If it isn't in here, say so." Then spot-check two or three figures, because even with the document the AI can add things (see the Vectara numbers above).

Agents: what changes when AI acts, not just answers?

When AI only answers, a mistake is a bad paragraph you can delete; when an agent acts, a mistake can be an email sent, a payment made or a post published. So the rule changes: a person approves anything a customer sees, anything that moves money and anything that can't be undone.

An agent does things, not just talks. Its mistake can be a sent email or a payment. So a person must approve anything a customer sees, anything that moves money, and anything you can't undo.

An agent does things for you. It can send, buy or post. So you must say yes first to:

  • anything a customer sees
  • anything that spends money
  • anything you can't undo

Agents can be tricked by hidden words on a web page (Brave).

What agents still get wrong:

  • Doing the same job reliably. In tau-bench, a test of customer-service agents built by Sierra, a company that sells such agents, GPT-4o succeeded on fewer than half the tasks, and the chance of getting the same retail task right in 8 tries out of 8 was under 25% (Yao et al., 2024). It is an older model, but the lesson holds: right once is not right every time.
  • Following hidden instructions. Brave hid instructions in a Reddit comment. Asked only to summarise the page, Perplexity's Comet agent found the user's email address, triggered a one-time login code, read it from Gmail and posted both back. Brave's advice: "sending an email should always prompt the user to confirm right before the email is sent" (Brave, 20 Aug 2025).
  • Vendors' own numbers. Anthropic says its current Chrome setup cut attack success to under 0.08% in its own testing, and also says "You remain responsible for all browser actions taken by Claude performed on your behalf" (Anthropic). That is a vendor-run test.
  • Doing a job the same way every time. In one test by Sierra, a company that sells AI agents, an AI agent got the same store task right 8 times in a row less than 25% of the time (tau-bench).
  • Hidden instructions. Brave hid a message in a Reddit comment. An AI agent followed it, read a login code from Gmail and posted it (Brave).

Rules: who is responsible when AI is wrong?

You are. In both countries, the business that puts AI in front of customers owns what it says, under laws that already exist. This is general information, not legal advice.

You are. The business owns what its AI tells customers. This is not legal advice.

You are. Your chatbot is part of your business.

In Canada

  • In Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024), Air Canada argued its chatbot was "a separate legal entity". The BC Civil Resolution Tribunal rejected that, held the chatbot was part of Air Canada's website, and awarded CA$812.02 in total, which already includes interest and tribunal fees (CTV News; summary of 2024 BCCRT 149).
  • The Competition Act makes it reviewable conduct to make a public representation that is "false or misleading in a material respect", and a performance claim must rest on "an adequate and proper test" (Competition Act s. 74.01).
  • In September 2026 the Competition Bureau told the federal government there is "no AI exception": the rules cover what chatbots say, and labelling content as AI does not fix a misleading claim (BLG summary, 15 Oct 2026).
  • Canada's privacy commissioners say organisations using generative AI should take reasonable steps to check outputs, especially before they inform decisions about people or get published. This is guidance, not binding law (OPC).

In the US

  • The FTC's final order against Workado (28 Aug 2025) bars it from claiming how well its AI detector works without "competent and reliable evidence" at the time of the claim (FTC).
  • In a proposed policy statement (July 2026, not final), the FTC says ordinary hallucinations do not by themselves break the law it enforces, but a company "may, for example, unlawfully deceive consumers if it misrepresents the likelihood of such hallucinations" (Federal Register). So don't advertise that your chatbot "never makes mistakes".
  • Even an official bot can be wrong: New York City's own business chatbot gave answers that broke city and employment rules (AP via TechXplore). Check the rule at the source.
