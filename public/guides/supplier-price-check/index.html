Question 1 of 8

Why do supplier prices creep up without me noticing?

Because they rise in small steps, on single lines, and a weekly total hides them. In the US, producer prices for final demand rose 0.3% in October 2026 and 6.5% over the 12 months to October, while prices for processed goods that businesses buy from each other rose 14.6% over the same 12 months (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, released November 13, 2026). In Canada, the Industrial Product Price Index was up 12.6% year over year in October, its 25th straight month of year-over-year increases (Statistics Canada, November 19, 2026).

Owners feel it. Rising costs were the most common financial challenge in the Federal Reserve Banks' survey of US small employer firms: 77% reported rising costs, tariff-related costs or both. Among firms that buy some inputs from abroad, 76% passed at least some of the higher costs on to customers and 60% absorbed at least some (Federal Reserve Banks, 2026 Report on Employer Firms). In Canada, 45% of small firms named product input and material costs as a main cost constraint in November 2026 (a CFIB survey of its members, 565 responses, CFIB Business Barometer).

Three things hide a rise on an invoice:

  • The step is small. A 3% move on one line doesn't change the total enough to catch your eye.
  • The pack changes. "A case of 40 instead of 48 at the same price is a 20% rise per unit," as one Canadian restaurant software maker puts it (Tasti, a vendor's guide).
  • A new charge appears. A fuel surcharge or delivery fee on its own line raises what you pay without touching any product price.

What do I need before I start?

Two prices for each item you buy often: what you paid last time and what this invoice says, both with the pack size. You also need roughly how much of it you use in a month. Start with your top 20 to 30 lines by spend, not everything.

Where those come from depends on how your suppliers bill you and what you run your business on:

  • Supplier invoices. Paper, PDF by email, or a download from the supplier's ordering site. A spreadsheet or CSV download is better than a PDF, and a PDF is better than a photo.
  • Your accounting software. QuickBooks Online and Xero both export bills and reports as spreadsheet files, but the cheaper plans limit what you can track. In Canada, QuickBooks Online's bill management, purchase orders and inventory tracking come only with Plus (CA$110 a month list price) and Advanced; Lite and EasyStart don't include them (QuickBooks Canada). Xero's Canadian Starter plan (CA$25 a month) includes entering 5 bills, so check the limit if you get supplier bills every week; Standard is CA$60 (Xero Canada).
  • Your till. Square exports reports as CSV files from Reports in the Square Dashboard (Square). Toast's basic system counts items, not ingredients; its xtraCHEF add-on, which includes an ingredient price tracker, is listed at $179 a month per location for Essentials and $279 for Pro on Toast's US support page (Toast).

In the US

Compare prices before sales tax. If a supplier sometimes charges tax and sometimes doesn't, that's a question for the supplier, not a price change.

In Canada

Compare prices before GST/HST and PST, because the same price lands differently by province: GST is 5%, HST is 13% in Ontario, 14% in Nova Scotia (since April 1, 2025) and 15% in New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador and PEI; British Columbia and Manitoba add 7% PST, Saskatchewan 6% and Quebec 9.975% QST (Canada Revenue Agency). How does a 15-minute price check work?

Four steps: work out the price per unit for each line, compare it with last time, flag anything up 3% or more, and rank the flags by what they cost you in a month. The price per unit is the pack price divided by the number of units in the pack (litres, kilograms or each). The monthly cost of a change is the change per unit times the units you use in a month. Accountants call the dollar figure a purchase price variance: "(Actual price - Standard price) x Actual quantity" (AccountingTools, September 2, 2026).

The 3% line is a house rule, not a standard. I use 3% because it's high enough to skip normal noise and low enough to catch a rise worth a phone call. Pick your own and keep it the same every week.

How do I build it myself in ChatGPT or Claude?

Give it last time's prices and this week's invoice lines as a spreadsheet, ask it to do the maths in code, and ask for the same table every week. Both ChatGPT and Claude read spreadsheets on their free plans, and Claude's free plan can run code. Save the instructions once so next week is a single message.

Set up your file with the same 10 columns as the tool, one row per item, headings in the first row: item, supplier, unit, last pack, last units per pack, last price, new pack, new units per pack, new price, units used a month. OpenAI's own advice for data files is the same: "Include descriptive column headers in the first row" and "Use one row per record", with no empty rows or columns or second table on the same sheet (OpenAI Help Center). You can copy the made-up Laneway Coffee file from the tool above to practise.

The prompt (words in [brackets] are for you to fill in):

Prompt

I run [business], a [type of business] in [city]. Attached are last time's supplier prices and this week's invoice lines, one row per item. Do the maths in code, not in your head. For each line: work out the price per unit (per litre, kilogram or each) for last time and now, the % change, and the monthly impact (change per unit x units used a month). Flag every line up [3]% or more, every new charge, and every line where the pack size changed. Give me one table sorted by monthly impact, biggest first, then the total for the flagged lines. List any line you couldn't match or any number you had to guess.

How do I check the AI didn't get the numbers wrong?

Check it against the invoice, not against the AI. AI tools still make mistakes on exactly this kind of work. In a benchmark posted in November 2026 (a preprint with strict pass-or-fail grading, by authors whose company sells evaluation services), the best AI tested passed only 38.3% of its attempts at 70 realistic workplace spreadsheet requests (GDP.xlsx, arXiv). In a data company's own test on finance data, one model counted 13 duplicated invoice lines twice and "12 runs in a row answered 16,836 instead of 16,810", and another "put 88.47% of value in Czech koruna, whose real share is 1.34%", by applying an exchange rate backwards (Dataddo, a vendor-run test). The Government of Canada's own guide lists arithmetic among the "tasks that research shows they don't do well" (Government of Canada).

Four checks before you act on a flag:

  1. Tie it to the invoice. The lines in the table should add up to the invoice total before tax. If they don't, a line is missing or doubled.
  2. Check the flagged lines by hand. For each flag, divide the pack price by the pack size yourself. It takes a minute.
  3. Look for pack changes. Any line where the pack size changed gets a second look, both ways: the cups and the sugar in the example above.
  4. Ask what it couldn't match. An item with a new name or code can look like a new product. Running the same check twice and getting the same answer isn't proof: Dataddo's duplicate-line mistake repeated 12 times in a row.

Question 7 of 8

What do I do when I find a price jump?

Ask before you accept, and ask for one thing. Treat a rise as the start of a conversation with your supplier: confirm which items, how much and from when, ask what's driving it, and ask for one concession, such as more notice, the old price on orders already placed, or a better price for a bigger order. Then get whatever you agree in writing.

Start with the lines at the top of the table, because that's where the money is. In the example, oat milk and the new fuel surcharge are about half the flagged total.

Your choices for each flagged line:

  • Ask. A short, polite email to your rep about the specific line. I draft these with AI and send them myself.
  • Shop it. Get one other quote for the same item, pack and delivery, and compare the delivered cost per unit, not the case price.
  • Change what you buy. A different size, brand or spec, if quality holds.
  • Change your price. If the rise is real and lasting, re-cost the item it goes into and decide whether your menu or shelf price moves.
