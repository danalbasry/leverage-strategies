Question 1 of 9

Is this workshop for me, and what will I walk out with?

It's for you if you run or manage a small business, you've tried AI a little or not at all, and you want it doing real work by next week. You don't need any technical skill, and you don't need to pay for anything to take part. You do need a laptop, a free account on one AI tool set up before the day, and three things written down: your refund or booking policy, a one-page fact sheet for this week, and five jobs that come back every week. I send the steps and a checklist a week ahead.

You walk out with:

  • Three jobs set up. One saved workspace (a Project in ChatGPT or Claude, or a skill in Gemini) for each of your three jobs, holding your own facts and instructions so next week starts where you left off. Each win block ends with five minutes to save your own version.
  • One checking routine. Four checks you run on anything the AI gives you before it goes anywhere.
  • One small agent, tried safely. A small job you hand to an agent yourself, watching where it stops for your approval.
  • A plan for the week after. The day you'll do your first job, a partner from the room, and a 15-minute check-in a week later.
  • The one-page handout at the end of this page.

Who it isn't for. If you want custom software built, or AI making decisions about customers, staff or money without a person checking, this isn't the place to start. If your business handles health records or other sensitive personal information, you're still welcome, but every exercise uses made-up data and we talk about your rules before you touch real files.

Question 3 of 9

Which three jobs make the best first wins?

Write it, answer customers, and summarize your numbers. Writing is the top use among small businesses that use AI, and all three are jobs where a person can check the result before it goes anywhere.

The surveys agree on the first one. Among US small employer firms that use AI, 83% use it for writing or marketing, 61% for individual productivity and 51% for planning or analysis (Federal Reserve Banks, 2026 Report on Employer Firms). In Canada, the most common uses among businesses using AI are data analytics (36.6%), text analytics (34.5%) and virtual agents or chatbots (28.2%) (Statistics Canada).

What owners don't do yet matters too. Only 6% of US small business workers who use AI use it mainly to automate workflows with minimal human involvement; 64% use it for personal productivity, such as drafting, summarizing and brainstorming (an Ipsos survey commissioned by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce Foundation). And only 7% of AI-using US firms said AI was fully built into their business (Federal Reserve Banks). That's why the first wins are drafts a person checks, not automations that run on their own.

But your three jobs come from your week, not from a survey. In block 2 everyone lists the jobs that come back every week and scores each one the way I do with clients: hours per week times how easy the result is to check (3 easy, 2 medium, 1 hard). Then the safety gate: a job waits for later if the AI's answer would reach a customer with no one reading it, if it moves money or changes records, or if it involves hiring, health or legal advice. The top three that pass are your three.

Question 5 of 9

How does each exercise run?

Each exercise uses the same prompt shape, so you only learn one (words in [brackets] are for you to fill in): what the business is, what to do, the facts to use, and what the answer should look like. That shape is what all four vendors recommend in their own guides. OpenAI says to treat a prompt "like a 'handoff' to a new intern" (OpenAI); Anthropic says to think of Claude "as a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows" (Anthropic); Google says to provide context and break big jobs into smaller prompts (Google); Microsoft names four parts: context, goal, source and expectations (Microsoft).

Exercise 1: answer customers (30 minutes)

You turn a customer message into a reply you check and send yourself. The AI never sends anything.

The prompt (fill in the brackets):

Prompt

I run [business], a [type of business] in [city]. Our policy: [for example, refunds within 14 days with a receipt; we never argue in public replies]. Draft a reply to the customer message below. Warm, and offer [one concrete fix]. Use only the facts I've given you. If the message asks something my policy doesn't cover, say so instead of guessing. Give me two versions: a public reply under 50 words and a private reply under 120 words. End each with one line starting "Check before sending:".

Customer message: [paste a made-up message, or a real one with the name, email, phone and order number removed]

In ChatGPT

  1. Create a Project for the job and add your policy as a file or in the instructions. Projects are on the Free plan (ChatGPT).
  2. Paste the prompt in a chat inside the Project.
  3. Edit in the chat ("Shorter. Keep the offer."), then copy the reply and send it yourself.

In Claude

  1. Click Projects, then New Project. Add your policy and two replies you've liked as examples. The Free plan allows up to 5 Projects (Claude).
  2. Paste the prompt. Ask Claude to quote the policy line it relied on, so you can check it.
  3. Copy the reply out and send it yourself.

In Gemini

  1. Paste the prompt in a new chat.
  2. To reuse it, save the instructions as a skill. Don't build a Gem: Google says Gems are going away on personal accounts and moving to skills from November 2026 (Google).

In Copilot

Paste the prompt into Copilot Chat. Microsoft's own guide asks you to give context, a goal, a source and your expectations, which is the same shape (Microsoft).

Exercise 2: a week of posts from your own facts (35 minutes)

You write seven posts in which every fact comes from a one-page sheet you supply. The rule is the lesson: the AI may only use your facts.

You bring the sheet as pre-work: your business name, what you sell, this week's real items with prices, one event with a date, one offer with an end date, and your hours. Here's a made-up sheet we use as a sample in the room:

Made-up example business

Harbour Street Bakery

Fact sheet for this week

  • Sourdough loaf $9.
  • Cinnamon buns $4.50 each, $24 for six.
  • Holiday cookie boxes $35, order by December 18.
  • Open Tuesday to Sunday, 7 am to 3 pm.
  • Saturday December 12: free coffee tasting 9 to 11 am.

The prompt:

Prompt

Here is my fact sheet: [paste it]. Write 7 posts, one for each day, Monday to Sunday. Each post: one fact only, under 60 words, one call to action, no more than 3 hashtags. Use ONLY the facts on the sheet. If a post needs a fact that isn't there, write [MISSING FACT] instead of making one up. Give me a table: Day, Post, Fact used.

Then check every row: the "Fact used" column must match your sheet. With the sample sheet, a post that mentions Monday hours or a price for coffee has invented something, because neither is on the sheet.

The two-tool version (15 minutes, research started first). One tool finds the information, another turns it into the finished thing, and you check in between. This is how I work myself. Research tools can take several minutes, so you start the research at the beginning of the block and write your seven posts while it runs.

  1. Research. Ask a research tool: "What are [businesses like yours] in [your city] posting about for the holidays this year? Give me a short report with a link for every fact. Don't invent prices."
  2. Check. Open two or three of the links. Cross out anything you can't confirm.
  3. Write. Paste the checked research and your fact sheet into ChatGPT or Claude: "Using ONLY the research and facts below, write one post for my business. Keep the source link for every claim. If something isn't answered, write [NOT FOUND]."
  4. Check again before you post.

In Gemini

Gemini's free plan includes Deep Research (Google). It's my default research tool in a Canadian room, because the free plan and its Canadian price are on Google's own Canadian page.

In Muse

If you already use Meta's Muse agent, it can do the research step. Meta says Muse is free for most uses (iPhone in Canada). Muse for Small Business connects to Instagram and Facebook Pages, and Meta says nothing is published, sent or spent without your approval.

In ChatGPT

Use ChatGPT's search for the research step, then a new chat inside your post Project for the writing step, so the two jobs stay separate.

Exercise 3: summarize a file, then check it (30 minutes)

You ask AI to summarize a sales file, then check its answer against an answer key I worked out in code. Everyone uses the same made-up file: 31 days of October sales for Laneway Coffee, a made-up Toronto café, with the weather and notes for each day. It's the sample file from my worked example of a café's first month [LINK TO THE CAFÉ ARTICLE].

The prompt:

Prompt

Here are last month's daily sales for my café. In plain words, what's going on, and what are 3 things I should try next week? Show the numbers you used. Compare weekdays with weekdays and weekends with weekends. Leave out unusual days, like holidays or road work, and say which ones. Tell me anything the file can't answer.

In ChatGPT

Attach the CSV file to a chat and paste the prompt. Then ask: "Before finishing, list any assumptions you made." That line comes from OpenAI's own prompting guide (OpenAI).

In Claude

Click the + button, attach the file and paste the prompt (Claude Help Center). Ask Claude to work out the totals with code, so the arithmetic is computed, not guessed.

In Gemini

Open the file in Google Sheets, click Ask Gemini at the top right and paste the prompt. It needs an eligible Workspace or Google AI plan (Google). On a free account, upload the file to a Gemini chat instead.

In Copilot

Attach the file in Copilot Chat and paste the prompt. Copilot inside Excel needs a paid Microsoft 365 plan.

The answer key. I worked these out in code from the 31 rows:

Check What a good answer says
Total$40,056.74 (CAD) from 4,110 transactions
WeekendsA normal dry weekend day took in about 70% more than a normal dry weekday ($2,033.88 against $1,197.63)
RainRainy weekdays took in about 21% less than normal dry weekdays
The trapAveraging all rain days against all dry days gives about 15%, because two rain days were Saturdays and the dry days include Canadian Thanksgiving (October 12) and a week of road work

How do we check what the AI gave us?

Check it against something you trust, because AI tools still make mistakes on exactly this kind of work. In a benchmark posted in November 2026 (a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, with strict pass-or-fail grading), the best AI tested passed only 38.3% of its attempts at 70 realistic workplace spreadsheet requests (GDP.xlsx, arXiv). The authors' company, Surge AI, sells evaluation services. And when models summarize a document, even the best on Vectara's leaderboard adds something the document didn't say in 1.8% of summaries; GPT-4o does it in 9.6% and Claude Opus 5.5 in 9.9% (Vectara). Vectara sells AI search tools and its own model does the judging, so read that as a vendor-run test. US owners already know this: accuracy was the top challenge AI users reported, named by 46% (Federal Reserve Banks).

Four checks I teach in every room:

  1. Check numbers against the source, not against the AI. Open the file and compare the totals. For the sample file: $40,056.74 from 4,110.
  2. Ask which rows, pages or links it used. If it can't say, don't use the answer. The Government of Canada's own guide says the same: "Don't use generative AI tools as search engines unless sources are provided so that you can verify the content" (Government of Canada).
  3. Compare like with like. Weekdays against weekdays, this month against the same month. The 15% rain answer fails this check.
  4. Ask what it can't answer. If it gives advice the facts can't support, it's guessing.

Start with low-stakes jobs. The same Canadian guide gives a useful line: start with low-risk uses, such as an internal email or a draft that a person reviews, before higher-risk ones, such as a public chatbot or a summary of client information (Government of Canada). That's the order of the three wins.

What happens the week after?

The week after decides whether any of this sticks, so it's planned before you leave. Before the last block ends, everyone writes down one job, the day they'll do it, and the name of a partner from the room. A week later we meet for 15 minutes online and each person shows one thing they did.

Here's the evidence behind that design, and how strong it is:

  • Skills fade without use. A 2025 review of 457 reports found that, without use, half of the accuracy people gained in training was lost after about 6.5 months (Tatel and Ackerman, Psychological Bulletin). That study is about practical skills in general, not AI, so read it as a warning, not a forecast.
  • Peers matter. In two studies of more than 1,000 employees in Germany and the US, published in July 2026, peer support and feedback mattered both for using what people learned and for passing it on to colleagues (Mehner, Rothenbusch and Kauffeld, Frontiers in Organizational Psychology).
  • Start from a real job. After OpenAI's Small Business Jam with 530 owners in five US cities, its first lesson was "Start with workflow-first learning", and another was "Use peer community to accelerate learning." 78% left with a working workflow and 92% said they'd apply what they learned within 30 days. That's OpenAI's own report, and those are same-day answers, not measured use (OpenAI).
  • Training time and coaching. In BCG's survey of more than 10,600 workers in 11 countries, people who got five or more hours of training, especially in person and with coaching, were "significantly more likely to become regular users". Only 36% felt adequately trained (BCG). BCG sells AI consulting, so that's its own survey.

The 15-minute check-in itself isn't something a study has tested at that exact timing. It's my design, built on the peer and feedback evidence above.
