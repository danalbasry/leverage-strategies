Question 1 of 9

Which jobs in a restaurant is AI actually good at today?

Writing and marketing first, then reading feedback, then scheduling. Those are the jobs operators report using AI for most, and they're all jobs where a person can check the result before a guest sees it. Very few restaurants let AI take orders.

The surveys point the same way. In the National Restaurant Association's November 2026 survey of 212 US operators, 38.6% said they use AI tools in some form, but only 3.2% were implementing AI across the company and 20% were running small pilots. Among businesses using AI, the most common jobs were personalizing marketing and customer outreach (58.3%), analyzing customer feedback (47.7%), recruiting, scheduling or labour management (37.1%) and forecasting sales or demand (34.3%). Taking orders and processing payments came in at 20.0% (National Restaurant Association). The survey was sponsored by DoorDash and PayPal, and the most-wanted help was training and education (56.5%).

In Canada, only 16.3% of workers in accommodation and food services used generative AI at work in the past year, the lowest of any industry, against 35.9% of all workers (Statistics Canada, March 2026 survey).

What restaurants ask AI also tells you where it helps. Toast says more than 125,000 US locations used its built-in assistant, Toast IQ, in the first quarter of 2026. The top topics were sales and revenue (47% of restaurants using it), menu and inventory (34%) and guests and marketing (32%), and the most common prompt was "Create a short, easy-to-read daily briefing for my restaurant" (Toast via Business Wire). That's Toast's own usage data, not a test of how good the answers were.

What no one has shown yet. I couldn't find a single independent test of AI doing a specific restaurant job better, with a control group. So read every number in this section as "operators report using AI for this", not "AI is proven at this". I couldn't find any survey that breaks out bakeries, food trucks or takeout-only kitchens either, so the table below is my own read of where each one's time goes.

Question 2 of 9

What should I try first, in 30 minutes?

Rewrite three menu descriptions from your own recipe cards, then check every word against your allergen matrix. It's the best first job because you already have the facts, the result is easy to check, and the check itself teaches you where AI goes wrong.

You need three things: the recipe card for each dish (every ingredient, including what's in the sauces and anything bought in), the current price, and an allergen matrix, which is a simple table with your dishes down the side and the allergens across the top. If you don't have a matrix yet, the AI can help you build one from the recipe cards, and you then check it against the supplier labels.

Here's the prompt (fill in the brackets):

Prompt
I run [restaurant name], a [type of restaurant] in [city]. Below are recipe cards with prices for 3 dishes. For each dish, write a menu description of 25 words or fewer. Use ONLY the ingredients on the card. Don't add origins, farms, cooking methods or claims like "local", "house-made" or "free from" unless they are on the card. Keep the price exactly as written. Under each description, write one line starting "Contains:" that lists every allergen from my allergen matrix for that dish, using the [US / Canadian] list. Then give me a table: dish, every ingredient word you used, and whether it is on the card (yes or no).

[Paste recipe cards, prices and allergen matrix]

Question 3 of 9

How do I write a menu and menu descriptions with AI?

Keep one master file with every dish, price and ingredient, let the AI write only from that file, and have the person who wrote the recipe check every description before it's printed. The rules about what must be on a menu mostly don't apply to a single independent location, with one big exception in Quebec.

My setup for menu work. One Project (in ChatGPT or Claude) holds the master menu file, the recipe cards and the allergen matrix, plus standing instructions: the word limit, your tone, a list of words you never want ("artisanal", "elevated", "mouthwatering"), and the rule "use only the facts in the files". Prices live in the file, never in the prompt. When a supplier changes a product or you change a price, update the file first, then ask the AI to list every description affected. Date the file each time.

Who checks. The cook who wrote the recipe checks ingredients. The person who sets prices checks numbers. If that's the same person, check them in two passes, not one.

What the menu rules are, and which apply to you.

In the US

  • Calorie labelling on menus applies to restaurants that are part of a chain with 20 or more locations operating under the same name with substantially the same menu (FDA). A single independent location isn't covered. FDA lists a voluntary registration form for businesses that choose to follow the rules.
  • California's SB 68 requires food facilities with 20 or more locations to give written notice of the nine major allergens in each menu item from July 1, 2026, with an alternative format if the menu is digital (California State Senate). Other states have bills in progress, so check your own state.

In Canada

  • Ontario's Healthy Menu Choices Act applies to food service premises with 20 or more locations in Ontario (Government of Ontario). A single location isn't covered.
  • Quebec is the exception that does apply to one location. Section 51 of the Charter of the French Language says the French-language rule "applies also to menus and wine lists", and that a translation is allowed but "no inscription in another language may be given greater prominence than that in French" (LégisQuébec). With AI, write the French menu first, then the English, and check the English is never bigger or easier to find.
  • Tool prices in this guide are before tax. In Canada, add GST/HST (and PST or QST where it applies).

Where each tool fits. ChatGPT and Claude are the simplest for menu writing, and both have free plans with Projects. Gemini fits if your menus already live in Google Docs and Sheets. Copilot fits only if you already pay for Microsoft 365 and keep your menu in Word.

How do I plan specials and posts each week?

Decide the specials yourself, give the AI only those facts, and post nothing until you've read it. AI is good at turning "squash soup, $9, Thursday to Sunday" into a week of posts in your voice. It's bad at knowing what's actually on.

A Monday routine (about 30 minutes):

  1. Write three lines: this week's specials with prices and days, what needs using up, and anything off the menu.
  2. Paste them into your posts Project with this prompt:
Prompt
Here are this week's specials: [paste]. Write 5 posts for Instagram and Facebook, one per day from [day] to [day]. Each post: one special, its price and the days it's on, under 50 words, one call to action, no more than 3 hashtags. Use ONLY these facts. If a post needs a fact that isn't here, write [MISSING FACT]. Never mention allergens or "free from" in a post. Give me a table: day, post, fact used.
  1. Check every row against your three lines. Then make the picture from a real photo (see the next section) and schedule each post yourself in the scheduler you already use.

Question 6 of 9

How do I keep allergens safe when AI touches the menu?

The AI never decides what's safe. It formats what your allergen matrix already says, and a named person checks every allergen line against the recipe card and the supplier label before it reaches a guest. AI often sounds right on these questions even when it isn't, and its mistakes are confident and specific, which is exactly what makes them dangerous.

The evidence. In a 2025 peer-reviewed test, ChatGPT answered 120 allergy questions; more than half of the answers contained some error and critical errors appeared in 5% of all responses, including a broad recommendation of hypoallergenic formulas for children with confirmed cow's milk allergy that, without clarification, could lead to life-threatening anaphylaxis. The authors concluded ChatGPT "is not reliable for unsupervised use in clinical allergy contexts" (AAAAI summary of Mathes et al., J Allergy Clin Immunol Pract). That's medical questions, not menus, and I found no current test of AI on restaurant menu allergens. That gap is itself the finding: no one has shown these tools are safe for this job.

Use the right list.

In the US

  • FDA's nine major food allergens are milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame; sesame became the ninth on January 1, 2023. FDA also warns that some manufacturers changed recipes after that, so "there are now food products with small amounts of sesame that did not have it before" (FDA). Check supplier labels on every delivery.
  • The FDA Food Code is a model your state may or may not have adopted. Where the 2022 edition applies, unpackaged food needs "written notification to consumers of the presence of major food allergens", which can be on the menu, a table tent, a placard or electronic, and staff training must cover the nine allergens (FDA factsheet). As of December 31, 2025, 14 states had adopted the 2022 edition, covering 25.60% of the US population (FDA). Ask your local health department which edition applies to you.

In Canada

  • The CFIA's priority allergens are eggs, milk, mustard, peanuts, crustaceans and molluscs, fish, sesame seeds, soy, sulphites, tree nuts, and wheat and triticale; gluten sources (barley, oats, rye, triticale, wheat) must also be identified (CFIA). Those rules are for prepackaged food. If you package food yourself to sell, read the CFIA page carefully.
  • For plated food, a Toronto study of 1,000 independent restaurants' online menus states: "In Canada, there are no laws requiring allergen labeling on non-pre-packaged food." Only 16% of menus had an allergen statement and 10% used allergen symbols (Nahle et al., Journal of Food Protection).
  • Provinces set training rules. In Ontario, at least one food handler or supervisor who has completed food handler training must be on the premises during every hour it's open (O. Reg. 493/17, s. 32). In Quebec, any allergen note on the menu needs to be in French too.

My safeguards for any menu AI touches:

  1. One source of truth. The allergen matrix, owned by one named person, updated on every recipe change, supplier change or substitution.
  2. Give the AI the matrix, not the question. "Is this dish nut-free?" invites a guess. "Format this matrix into menu symbols" keeps it inside your facts.
  3. Search every draft for "free". Free of, free from, nut-free, gluten-free, dairy-free, vegan. Every hit gets checked by a person, every time.
  4. Name the list. US list in the US, Canadian list in Canada. If in doubt, use the Canadian list, which is the broader one.
  5. Log it. Date, matrix version, who checked, which supplier label. If something goes wrong, that record matters.
  6. Never let an AI answer a guest's allergy question. Not on the phone, not in chat, not in a review reply. A person answers, from the matrix.

In the US
