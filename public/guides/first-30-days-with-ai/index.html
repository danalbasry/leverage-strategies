Question 1 of 8

What will I be able to do after 30 days?

You'll have a short list of jobs AI now helps you with every week, a prompt for each one saved where you can find it, and notes on how long each took. You'll also know which tool suits you, what never goes into it, and how to catch it when it's wrong. You won't have a habit yet, and that's normal: in the best-known study of habit forming, it took people 66 days on average to reach full automaticity, with a range of 18 to an estimated 254 days, and a single missed day made little difference (British Psychological Society, on Lally and colleagues). So treat these 30 days as the start, not the finish.

You're not alone in starting now. About half of US adults (49%) have used an AI chatbot, up from a third in 2024, and 24% use one daily (Pew Research Center, survey of 5,119 adults, February 2026). Among US small businesses that use AI, 83% use it for writing or marketing (Federal Reserve Banks). That's why the first week is about words.

In the US

Small businesses are still early. The Federal Reserve Banks' survey of 6,525 employer firms found 46% use AI, and only 7% of the firms using it said it was fully built into their business (Federal Reserve Banks). That survey is a convenience sample, so read it as a picture, not a census.

In Canada

In a 2025 CFIB survey (a business owners' association's own survey) of 1,683 small business owners, 41% used generative AI but only 18% used it daily. Of those using it, 84% used it to create content such as emails and marketing copy. The top barriers were lack of digital skills (51%), lack of time (49%) and high costs (48%) (CFIB). Statistics Canada counts AI use differently, so its number is lower: 19.2% of Canadian businesses used AI in the second quarter of 2026 (Statistics Canada). Don't mix the two numbers.

Which free tool should I start with?

Start with the tool you already have an account for, because the first week should be about the work, not about sign-ups. If you use Gmail, start in Gemini. If you use Outlook or Microsoft 365, start in Copilot. If you have neither, or want a general assistant, start in ChatGPT or Claude. Pick one and stay with it for the first two weeks; you'll try a second tool in week 4.

ChatGPT is the most used by far (44% of US adults have used it), followed by Gemini (24%), Copilot (17%), Meta AI (14%), Grok (8%) and Claude (6%) (Pew Research Center). Being popular doesn't make it right for you; where your work already lives matters more.

Question 3 of 8

What should I never type into it, and what do I change first?

Never type anything you wouldn't want stored, used to train the AI, or shown to someone else, and switch off model training on day 1 before your first real task. Canada's privacy commissioner lists the risks plainly: what you type may be collected and kept, used to train the AI, used to build a profile of you, and possibly revealed to other users (Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada).

The never-type list. Passwords and login codes. Card, bank and government ID numbers (SIN or SSN). Health information. Anything about a child, and never a child's photo. Customers' and staff members' names, contact details and records. Anything under a confidentiality agreement. When you practise, use a made-up name and change the details, which is the commissioner's own advice.

Day 1 setup, 10 minutes. Switch off training, and learn where the private chat is.

In ChatGPT

Settings, then Data controls: turn off "Model improvement". For anything sensitive, use a Temporary Chat, which won't appear in your history or help improve OpenAI's models (OpenAI).

In Claude

Settings, then Privacy: turn off "Model Improvement". Incognito chats aren't used to improve Claude even if Model Improvement is on (Anthropic).

In Gemini

Google says that, depending on your settings, your Gemini history is used to train its models, and a subset of chats is read by human reviewers and kept for up to three years. Turn History off, or use a Temporary chat, which isn't used for training (Google).

In Copilot

Look in your profile settings, under Privacy, for the model training switches and turn them off. [DAN: I COULDN'T OPEN MICROSOFT'S PRIVACY CONTROLS PAGE TODAY. CHECK THE EXACT SETTING NAMES ON A PERSONAL ACCOUNT BEFORE PUBLISHING.]

In Muse

Settings, then Data controls: turn off "Help improve our AI models" and confirm. It's on when you first use Muse, and turning it off also applies to your earlier chats (Meta Help Center).

Switching off training is not the same as deleting your history, and neither one deletes saved memories. If you want something gone, delete the chat too.

In the US

There's no federal law about what you type into a chatbot. The FTC's advice to consumers is short: "Verify any answers you get from a chatbot by searching online or checking with a reputable source. And don't rely solely on a chatbot for medical, legal, or financial advice" (FTC consumer alert, 2024). If you work with health records, the rules for your business still apply when you use AI: keep patient details out of consumer tools entirely.

Your business's own privacy duties (PIPEDA, or your province's law in Alberta, BC and Quebec) apply whatever tool you use (Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada). What do I do each day?

Each day you do one task from your own work, in about 10 minutes, and write one line in a log. Every task uses the same prompt shape, so you only learn one: who you are, what to do, the facts to use, and what the answer should look like. Words in [brackets] are for you to fill in.

Prompt

I run [business], a [type of business] in [city]. [What I want done.] Use only these facts: [paste the facts]. If something you need is missing, ask me instead of guessing. Keep it [length and tone].

The log. Keep it in a notebook or a spreadsheet. Fill it in honestly, including the days it didn't help.

If you hit the free limit, write LIMIT in the "What I had to fix" column.

Week 1: Ask and rewrite (days 1 to 7). Words first, because that's where most owners find their first use.

  1. Day 1: set up (tool, training off, a Project or skill called "My 30 days"). Then ask one question you'd normally search for.
  2. Day 2: paste an email you wrote this week (take out names and contact details first) and ask for a shorter, friendlier version. Edit it yourself.
  3. Day 3: ask it to explain something in your trade you find hard to explain to customers, in plain words a 12-year-old would follow.
  4. Day 4: paste a real customer question (name removed) and ask for a reply using only your policy.
  5. Day 5: upload a short document (a supplier's terms, a lease page) and ask for five bullet points, then check one point against the page.
  6. Day 6: take a photo of something at work (a label, a shelf, a form; nothing with a name or card on it) and ask what it shows and what to do next.
  7. Day 7: have a 5-minute voice conversation about your week. Then review: which day saved you time?

Week 2: Your week and your customers (days 8 to 14).

  1. Day 8: list this week's jobs and ask for a day-by-day plan, and what can move if a day slips.
  2. Day 9: paste messy notes and ask for a to-do list.
  3. Day 10: give it your facts for the week (hours, specials, an event) and ask for three short social posts. Check every fact.
  4. Day 11: write a product or service description from your own notes.
  5. Day 12: write replies to your three most common customer questions and save them.
  6. Day 13: ask for 10 subject lines or headlines for something you're promoting. Pick one.
  7. Day 14: review. Save the two prompts you'll reuse in your Project or skill.

Week 3: Your files and numbers (days 15 to 21).

  1. Day 15: upload two quotes or price lists and ask what's different. Check the totals yourself.
  2. Day 16: turn your common questions from day 12 into a one-page FAQ.
  3. Day 17: draft a short job post or a staff checklist.
  4. Day 18: upload a sales export with daily totals only (no customer names, emails or card details), or the sample file below, and ask which days were slow and why. Check it against the file.
  5. Day 19: plan a small promotion for a slow day, with a budget limit you set.
  6. Day 20: role-play a difficult customer call by voice, then ask what you could say better.
  7. Day 21: review. Which task would you pay someone to do?

Week 4: Repeat, measure, decide (days 22 to 30).

  1. Day 22: repeat your best task from week 1 and time it.
  2. Day 23: turn a job you repeat into a checklist or template.
  3. Day 24: ask it to find what's missing from your day 8 plan.
  4. Day 25: set up a second tool, switching training off first as on day 1, then try the same task and compare.
  5. Day 26: ask the AI what it can't do reliably for your kind of business, then check one claim.
  6. Day 27: write a short "how we use AI here" note for staff, including the never-type list.
  7. Day 28: review your log. Count the tasks you marked "use it again".
  8. Day 29: choose one daily use and one weekly use to keep.
  9. Day 30: decide: stay free, pay for one plan, or try one agent job (the next two sections help).

The 30 days are tasks, not dates. If you skip weekends, the plan takes about six weeks, and that's fine. If you miss a day, do the next one; one missed day made little difference in the habit research above.

Question 6 of 8

How do I know when it's wrong?

You check anything you'll act on against the original, because AI tools are often wrong while sounding sure. In a Columbia University test, eight AI search tools were asked to name the news article a quote came from, and they got it wrong in more than 60% of 1,600 tries; ChatGPT misidentified 134 of 200 articles and signalled doubt only 15 times. Paid versions answered more prompts correctly but also gave more confidently wrong answers, because they declined less often (Tow Center for Digital Journalism, March 2025). Paying more doesn't buy you out of checking.

How often it's wrong depends on the job. When you hand it a document and ask for a summary, the best models in one vendor-run benchmark added unsupported details to 1.8% of summaries, and widely used models such as Claude Opus 5.5 to about 10% (Vectara, updated November 24, 2026). On realistic spreadsheet requests, the best AI was fully correct in only 38.3% of attempts (GDP.xlsx, arXiv preprint; the authors' company sells evaluation services).

These are the four checks I teach in my workshop [LINK TO WORKSHOP 1]. Here they are for your own month.

Four checks for every task.

  1. Numbers against the source. Open the file or page and compare the totals yourself.
  2. Sources clicked. If it names a source, open it and find the line.
  3. Like with like. Weekdays against weekdays, this month against the same month. The 17% road-work answer fails this check.
  4. What's missing. Ask "what can't you answer from what I gave you?" A good answer names a gap.
