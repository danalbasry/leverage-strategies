Question 1 of 6

What happens in the first conversation, and what should I bring?

The first conversation is 60 minutes, and you do most of the talking. I'm not there to sell you software or audit your whole business. I'm there to understand how money and time move through it, and to find the jobs that repeat every week.

That shape isn't something I invented. SCORE, the US small business mentoring network, publishes a guide for a mentor's first session. It's a 60-minute agenda: setting expectations, the client's story, exploring the business, with the next meeting booked before you leave. Its advice to mentors is “Listen more than you speak. Resist the urge to immediately solve problems” (SCORE, Introductory Mentoring Session Guide). I run mine the same way, with one AI-specific part (your data and your rules) added before next steps.

Here's how the hour goes:

  1. Your story (10 to 15 minutes). What you sell, who buys it, and how the business makes money.
  2. Your next 6 to 12 months. What you're trying to get done, and what's in the way.
  3. Where the hours go. Which jobs come back every day or every week, who does them, and roughly how long they take.
  4. Your data and your rules. Where the information for those jobs lives (email, a spreadsheet, your point-of-sale system, paper), whether it can be exported, and what must never leave the building.
  5. Next steps. What I'll send you, what I need from you, and when we meet next.

What to bring. Bring what you have. Messy is fine.

  • A one-paragraph description of the business, or just be ready to tell it.
  • Any numbers you already keep: sales by month, hours, job counts, invoices outstanding.
  • A list of the three tasks you most wish someone else would do.
  • The logins or exports for the tools you use every day (we won't change anything in the first meeting).
  • Anything you've already tried with AI, including what went wrong.

After the conversation, I watch the work. In week 1 I spend [ON-SITE TIME, E.G. HALF A DAY] at your business, in person, with whoever does the jobs on your list. I watch the job being done, note every step and every place the information comes from, and ask where it goes wrong. Most of the useful detail only shows up when someone does the job in front of you.

Question 2 of 6

How do we choose which jobs to hand to AI first?

We pick jobs that repeat often, take real hours, and are easy for a person to check. Then we throw out anything risky, however good it looks.

The safe first jobs are the ones owners already use AI for. In the Federal Reserve survey, the most common use among firms using AI was writing or marketing (83%), then individual productivity (61%), then planning or analysis (51%) (Federal Reserve). In my experience, drafting, summarizing and sorting are where AI helps most today, and where mistakes are easiest to catch.

The measured studies point the same way. When a large software company gave its 5,179 customer support agents an AI tool that suggested replies, issues resolved per hour rose 14% on average, and 34% for newer, less experienced agents. The AI suggested; people still answered (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, NBER). That is the pattern I build around: AI drafts, a person decides.

How I score a job. I haven't found an official formula for this from any government or academic source, so treat this as my method, not a standard. For each job on your list I ask three things:

  1. Hours per week. Your best estimate for now. We measure it properly in week 1 to 2.
  2. How easy is it to check? Easy (3): a person reads the draft before it goes anywhere. Medium (2): checking takes some care. Hard (1): a mistake is hard to spot.
  3. Does it pass the safety gate? A job fails the gate if AI's answer would reach a customer with no one reading it, if it moves money or changes records, or if it involves hiring decisions, health information or legal advice. Jobs that fail the gate wait for round two, whatever their score.

Score = hours per week × ease of checking. The top three that pass the gate are your first three jobs.

A worked example. Here is the job list for a made-up business: Northside Plumbing, a 9-person plumbing and heating company. The hours are the kind of estimates an owner gives in a first conversation, not measured results.

Question 3 of 6

What gets built in weeks 2 and 3, and how long does it take?

For each of the three jobs I build one small, saved tool: usually a saved assistant that holds the instructions and reference files for that one job, sometimes a simple automation between two systems. I plan one to two hours to build and test each one.

A saved assistant for one job. In both ChatGPT and Claude this is called a Project. It keeps the job's instructions (how you write quotes, your tone, your rules) and your reference files (your price list, past quotes, your terms) in one place, so nobody has to paste them in each time.

In ChatGPT

  1. Open Projects in the left sidebar and create a new project. Give it the job's name, such as “Quotes”.
  2. Add your files, such as the price list and three good past quotes.
  3. Set the project instructions: what the job is, the format, and what to never do.
  4. On plans that support it, invite your team to the project.

Source: OpenAI Academy, Projects

In Claude

  1. Open Projects in the left sidebar (or go to claude.ai/projects) and click + New Project. Add a name and a description.
  2. Click Instructions, write how Claude should handle the job, and click Save instructions.
  3. Click + in the files panel to upload your files or connect Google Drive.
  4. On a Team or Enterprise plan, click Share project and add your team.

Source: Anthropic Academy, Introduction to projects

Skills aren't available on work accounts yet (Google, Gems to skills). I won't hand you something with an end date on it.

How long it really takes. I haven't found an independent study that measures build times for tools like these, so here's what I can honestly say. A first draft of a saved assistant takes under an hour. Getting it good enough that your team trusts it takes two or three rounds of testing on real work, so I plan one to two hours per job. That's my planning figure, not a measured one.

OpenAI reports the first version took “about two hours to build” and saves him “roughly 5-6 hours a week” (OpenAI Academy). That's the right size for a first build: one job, a couple of hours, a person still in charge.

Question 4 of 6

How do my staff learn to use it without it falling apart after I leave?

Your staff learn on their own work, in short sessions spread over the month, with one page of rules and one person on your team who becomes the go-to. A single lecture doesn't stick.

Training time matters, and most people don't get enough. In BCG's 2025 survey of more than 10,600 workers in 11 countries, only 36% said they'd been adequately trained, and people with five or more hours of training were much more likely to use AI regularly. BCG sells AI consulting, so treat this as a vendor survey (BCG, AI at Work 2025). The same survey found 54% would use AI tools even if they weren't approved. Some of your staff may use AI either way. The choice is whether they use the tools you set up, with your rules, or their own.

Here's how the training runs:

  • Session 1 (week 3, 45 minutes): each person does one real task with the tool, start to finish. Not a demo.
  • Session 2 (week 3, 30 minutes): we look at mistakes on purpose. I show where the tool gets things wrong, so people learn to check, not just trust.
  • Session 3 (week 4, 30 minutes): questions, fixes to the instructions, and the go-to person takes over.
  • Week 6 check-in (15 minutes): after I've gone, to see who's still using it and why.

That's about two hours of sessions, well short of the five hours BCG links to regular use. The rest comes from using the tools on real work every day in weeks 3 and 4, with the go-to person on hand. If your team needs more, we add sessions; I'd rather add time than leave a tool nobody uses.

One page of rules. In the KPMG and University of Melbourne global study (more than 48,000 people in 47 countries), only 30% of employees said their organization had a policy on generative AI, and 57% admitted relying on AI output without checking it (KPMG, Trust in AI 2025). So before anyone uses the tools, we agree one page:

  • Approved tools: the business's own paid account only, never staff members' personal accounts.
  • Green: public information (your website, your menu, your published prices) can go in any approved tool.
  • Yellow: internal documents (quotes, job notes) go only in the business account.
  • Red: never paste customer personal details, staff records, payment details, passwords or health information. If unsure, treat it as red.
  • A person checks anything AI helped write before it goes to a customer.
  • Report a mistake the same day. No one gets in trouble for reporting.

Question 5 of 6

How do we know it worked?

We count the same things before and after, the same way, and we trust the count over anyone's feeling, including mine.

This matters because people are bad at judging their own AI time savings. In a controlled study, 16 experienced software developers expected AI to make them 24% faster. Afterwards they believed it had made them 20% faster. The clock showed they were 19% slower (METR, arXiv). That was experts on hard coding work with early-2025 tools, so it won't match your business. But it's the clearest warning I know against “it feels faster”.

Even real time savings don't automatically turn into money. In a six-month experiment across 66 firms and 7,137 office workers, people who used an AI tool spent two fewer hours a week on email. The researchers found no change in how much other work they did or what kind (Dillon and others, arXiv). Saved time only counts when you decide where it goes.

For each job, we count:

  • How many times the job happened.
  • How many minutes each one took.
  • How often AI's draft needed big fixes.
  • Mistakes that reached a customer.
  • How many jobs used the new tool.

One week of “before” numbers is short. One month is too short to see more sales. So I won't promise that.
