Question 2

What marketing actually brings in local customers?

Reviews and your Google listing matter most.

In a US survey, almost everyone read reviews. About 9 in 10 people want the owner to reply (BrightLocal).

Answer every review, not just some. 8 in 10 people said they'd likely pick a business that answers them all. Half don't like copy-and-paste replies (BrightLocal). BrightLocal sells marketing tools, so this is their own survey.

Posting most weeks helps. A company that sells posting tools found that people who posted most weeks got about 5 times more likes and comments per post (Buffer). That's likes, not sales.

In Canada

I found no Canadian survey like this. In Canada, 2 in 3 owners using online tools said lack of time is their biggest problem (CFIB).

Reviews and your Google listing matter most. Posts come after them.

In a 2026 survey of about 1,000 US adults, almost everyone (97%) read reviews of local businesses. About 9 in 10 (89%) expect the owner to reply. About half (47%) won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews (BrightLocal). BrightLocal sells marketing software, so this is a company's own survey.

Replying to every review works better than replying to some. 80% said they'd likely use a business that answers all its reviews. Only 45% said that about one that answers only the good ones. And half said a copy-and-paste reply would put them off (BrightLocal). So we rewrite every AI draft in your own words.

Posting helps when you do it every week. Buffer, a company that sells posting tools, studied over 100,000 of its users. People who posted in most weeks got about 5 times more likes and comments per post than people who rarely posted (Buffer). That's likes, not sales. I found no study showing that more posts bring more sales, so I won't promise that.

In Canada

I found no Canadian survey like this one, so the numbers above are from the US. A 2025 survey of 2,478 Canadian owners found 59% use Facebook and 52% use Google Business Profile or Maps. Among owners using digital tools, 66% said lack of time was their biggest problem (CFIB).

Reviews and your Google listing come first, and posting comes after them. That's what the evidence supports, even though posting is what most owners spend their time on.

Reviews are checked by almost everyone. In BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults, 97% read reviews for local businesses, 89% expect owners to respond, and 47% won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews. Recency counts too: 74% look for reviews from the last three months (BrightLocal). BrightLocal sells local marketing software, so this is a vendor survey.

Replying to every review beats replying to some. In the same survey, 80% said they were likely to use a business that responds to all its reviews, against 45% for one that answers only positive reviews and 47% for one that answers only negative ones. And 50% said a templated or generic reply would put them off (BrightLocal). That last number is why every AI draft in the room gets rewritten in your own words.

AI is now part of how people find you. The share of BrightLocal's respondents using AI tools for local recommendations rose from 6% in 2025 to 45% in 2026 (BrightLocal). AI tools draw on the same public reviews and listings, so I expect this morning's work to help there too. That's my reasoning, not a measured result: I found no study that tests it.

Posting helps when it's steady. Buffer looked at more than 100,000 of its own users over 26 weeks: people who posted in 20 or more of those weeks got about 5 times the median engagement per post of people who posted in 4 weeks or fewer (Buffer). Buffer sells scheduling software and measured likes, comments and shares, not sales. I found no study showing that posting more brings a local business more sales, so I won't promise that.

In Canada

I found no Canadian survey like BrightLocal's on how customers find local businesses, so the numbers above are US numbers. On the business side, CFIB's September 2025 survey of 2,478 Canadian small business owners found 78% have a website, 59% use Facebook, 52% use Google Business Profile or Maps and 41% use Instagram. Among businesses using digital tools, 66% said lack of time was their biggest obstacle (CFIB). That's the problem this morning is built for.

So the morning runs in that order of value: posts first because they take the longest to draft, then reviews, then the Google profile, which most owners haven't touched in months.

You draft a month of posts in which every price, date and offer comes from your fact sheet, and anything missing is flagged instead of invented. Then you check every post.

You bring the fact sheet as pre-work. Here's the made-up one we use in the room:

Made-up example: Laneway Coffee, December fact sheet. Open Monday to Friday 7 am to 5 pm, Saturday and Sunday 8 am to 5 pm. December 24: 8 am to 2 pm. Closed December 25 and 26 and January 1. The check: spot what was invented. Before anyone checks their own month, the room checks five drafts I wrote with mistakes planted in them, against the Laneway fact sheet. These are made-up examples, not output from any tool.

  1. "Our gingerbread oat latte is back for $5.75. Come warm up."
  2. "Live music Saturday December 12, 10 to noon. Free entry for everyone!"
  3. "Boxing Day plans? We're open 8 to 2."
  4. "Buy a $50 gift card, get $10 more, all month long."
  5. "Pumpkin spice is still our number one. Grab one today."

Answer key. All five have a problem. (1) The price is $6.25, not $5.75. (2) The sheet doesn't say entry is free; it should read [CHECK]. Exercise 3: reply to your reviews, and ask for more (35 minutes)

You reply to every recent review, negatives first, and set up a way of asking for reviews that follows each platform's rules. On Google, AI can help you draft. On Yelp, you write it yourself.

Google's own advice for replies: keep it short, be conversational, not promotional, so no deals in a reply, address the reviewer by name and mention their specific feedback, never share a reviewer's private information, and for a complicated problem, ask them to contact you by phone or email (Google). Google's content policy also bans posting someone's full name or medical information without consent (Google).

The made-up reviews we practise on:

Made-up example

Made-up review, Google, 5 stars: "Best oat latte on the street, and the barista remembered my order. Priya S."

Made-up review, Google, 2 stars: "Waited 15 minutes on Saturday and my sandwich was cold. I told them about my nut allergy and nobody seemed to care. Mark T."

The prompt:

Prompt

I run [business] in [city]. Draft a reply to the Google review below. 2 to 3 sentences, warm and plain. Thank them by first name only. Mention [one specific detail from the review]. If something went wrong, apologise once, and say only what we will really do: [what you'll actually do]. Invite them to contact [phone or email] to talk it through. Do not offer a discount or anything in exchange. Do not repeat any health, allergy or personal details. Do not invent facts. Give me two versions.

Review: [paste the review, or a paraphrase with names and private details removed]

The 2-star review is the test. A good reply thanks Mark, apologises for the wait and the cold sandwich, and asks him to call. It does not mention his allergy in public. A reply that says "we take nut allergies seriously" repeats a health detail on a public page.

In Gemini

On Yelp, write it yourself. Yelp says reviews drafted or revised with third-party AI tools aren't allowed, though spell-check and grammar tools are (Yelp). That post is about reviews. I couldn't open Yelp's full guidelines today to see how they treat owner replies, so my rule in the room is the careful one: on Yelp, the words are yours.

Asking for reviews. The two big platforms want opposite things.

  • Google: ask every customer the same way. Google gives every profile a review link and QR code (on a computer: Read reviews, then Get more reviews) and suggests putting it on receipts, in thank-you emails and in the shop (Google). Never offer anything for a review, never ask only happy customers, never pressure people on the premises, and never have staff review the business (Google).
  • Yelp: don't ask at all. Yelp says "Proactively asking for reviews may hurt your Yelp rating" and not to ask customers, mailing lists, friends or family (Yelp).

In Canada

A review request sent by email or text counts as a commercial electronic message under CASL. You need consent, your business's identification, and an unsubscribe that works within 10 business days (CRTC). A card at the till or a QR code on the counter needs none of that.

In Quebec

If a customer comments in French, you must reply in French (Éducaloi). Keep a French version of your reply drafts, and check AI's French with someone who speaks Quebec French before posting.

Exercise 4: update your Google Business Profile (30 minutes)

You fix the parts of your profile customers check most: hours, description, categories and one dated post. Changes are made signed in to the Google account that manages the profile.

The checklist, from Google's own help pages (Google):

  1. Name: exactly as on your sign. Nothing added.
  2. Categories: the most specific primary category, plus up to 9 more that are true.
  3. Hours: regular hours, then special hours for every holiday. For Laneway Coffee: December 24, 8 am to 2 pm; closed December 25, December 26 and January 1.
  4. Description: no more than 750 characters, no links, and about the business rather than promotions, prices or sales.
  5. Collected info: open Edit profile, then Collected info, and check anything Google gathered about you.
  6. One post: an Offer or Event post from your checked calendar, scheduled (Google).

The description prompt:

Prompt

Write a Google Business Profile description for my business using only these facts: [what you sell, who it's for, your neighbourhood, how long you've been open, what makes you different]. Under 700 characters, because Google's limit is 750. No links, no prices, no sales or offers. Then tell me the character count.

Count the characters yourself before pasting: the AI's count can be wrong. Google also has its own "Suggest description" button in some regions, and Google's warning on it is the rule for every tool: "This AI feature writes creative text. You should review it for accuracy and update as needed" (Google).

In the US

Since October 21, 2024, the FTC bans fake reviews, including AI-written ones from "someone who does not exist" (FTC). In December 2025 it warned 10 companies that fines can reach $53,088 per violation (FTC). Asking only happy customers "could violate the FTC Act" (FTC).

In Canada

Staff who review their own workplace must say so, "even if they're providing their honest opinion" (Competition Bureau). Advertised prices must include required fees, except government taxes (Competition Bureau). Marketing emails and texts follow CASL (CRTC). In Quebec, ads must normally be in French, with French at least twice as big as any other language (Éducaloi).

AI images. Use your own photos. If you use an AI image, check that site's labelling rules first.

Never write, buy or fake a review, and never hide a connection to the business. Both countries say so in law, and the platforms add their own rules on top. This is general information, not legal advice.

In the US

The FTC's Consumer Review Rule took effect October 21, 2024 (FTC). It bans fake reviews and testimonials, including ones that pretend to be "by someone who does not exist, such as AI-generated fake reviews", payment tied to a positive or negative review, undisclosed insider reviews, and suppressing negative reviews (FTC).

Asking only happy customers isn't banned by the rule itself, but the FTC says "this practice could violate the FTC Act" (FTC). Google bans it outright (Google).

On December 22, 2025, FTC staff sent warning letters to 10 companies about possible violations, noting civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation (FTC).

In Canada

There's no separate fake-review rule; the Competition Act's ban on false or misleading representations covers it. The Competition Bureau warned in January 2024 that employees must disclose their connection when they review their employer, "even if they're providing their honest opinion", and that a business that allows misleading reviews could be liable (Competition Bureau).

Advertised prices must include mandatory fees. The Bureau's guide to the June 2024 amendments says it is misleading to leave mandatory fees out of an advertised price, unless they are imposed by government, such as sales tax (Competition Bureau). Check that rule against every price in your month of posts.

CASL covers marketing emails and texts: consent, identification and an unsubscribe. Penalties can reach $10 million per violation for a business (CRTC).

In Quebec, advertising must normally be in French, and where another language appears, French must take at least twice the space (Éducaloi). Fines run from $3,000 to $30,000 per violation (Legal 500).

AI-made images. I didn't check each platform's current AI-labelling rules today, so the room uses your own photos for posts. If you use an AI image, check that platform's own help page on labels first.
