AI (artificial intelligence)

Software that works out an answer, a prediction or a draft from patterns in data, instead of only following rules someone typed in.

  • Why it matters: it is an umbrella word. "AI" on a product page tells you almost nothing until you ask which job it does.
  • Example: the shop's booking app guessing how busy Saturday will be from past bookings is AI.

Software that learns patterns from data and uses them to make a guess, an answer or a draft. It does more than follow fixed rules.

  • Why it matters: "AI" is a big umbrella word. On a product page, ask which job it actually does.
  • Example: the shop's booking app guesses how busy Saturday will be.

Software that finds patterns and makes a guess or a draft. It does more than follow fixed rules.

  • Why it matters: "AI" on a product page says very little. Ask what job it does.
  • Example: the shop's booking app guesses how busy Saturday will be.

Generative AI

AI that makes something new: text, images, audio, video or code. Canada's federal guide calls it "a type of AI that produces content such as text, audio, code, videos and images" (Government of Canada).

  • Why it matters: this is the kind you type to. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot and Muse are all generative AI tools.
  • Example: drafting the shop's reply to a customer who wants a quote for a new chain.

AI that makes new things: text, images, audio, video or code (Government of Canada).

  • Why it matters: this is the kind you type to. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot and Muse are all generative AI.

AI that makes new things. It can make text, pictures, sound, video or code (Government of Canada).

Chatbot

The window you type or talk to. Some chatbots are only menus of buttons. An AI chatbot answers in full sentences.

  • Why it matters: a chatbot on your website speaks for your business. Its wrong answer is your wrong answer.

Prompt

Everything you give the AI to work with: your question, your instructions and any material you paste in. Canada's guide says a prompt is "typically a short instructional text" (Government of Canada).

  • Why it matters: the AI does not know your business unless the prompt tells it.
  • Also: a system prompt is a set of instructions put in first, by the app maker or by you inside a project, that shapes every answer in that chat.
  • Example: "Reply to this customer using only the price list below. If the answer is not in it, say so."

How the maker built the model, by feeding it a huge amount of material long before you use it.

  • Why it matters: typing to a chatbot does not retrain it. When a vendor says "we trained it on your business", ask what they mean. Usually they gave it your documents to look up (grounding, below), which is cheaper and easier to change.

Training data

What the model learned from. Canada's federal guide warns that training data "could also be outdated" and "can also be biased" (Government of Canada).

  • Why it matters: the model may not know this year's prices, a new rule or the service you added last month.
  • Example: it cannot know Corner Bike Co. raised its tune-up price unless you tell it in the prompt.

What the AI learned from. Canada's guide warns it can be out of date and one-sided (Government of Canada).

Token

The small chunk of text an AI counts, often part of a word. Google says that for its Gemini models a token is about 4 characters, and 100 tokens is about 60 to 80 English words (Google AI for Developers).

  • Why it matters: usage limits and pay-as-you-go prices are counted in tokens, and each company counts a little differently, so never compare token numbers across tools.
  • In Canada: French usually takes more tokens than English. In one independent count of the same UN text, French used 252 tokens and English 189 with OpenAI's current encoding, about a third more (13k.eu, counted 6 Oct 2026). That is one text, so treat it as a direction, not a rule. A bilingual business hits limits sooner in French.

Context window

How much the AI can hold in mind at once in one chat, counted in tokens. Anthropic describes it as the model's "working memory", separate from what it was trained on, and notes that "as token count grows, accuracy and recall degrade" (Anthropic docs).

  • Why it matters: in a very long chat the AI can lose track of what you said at the start.
  • In ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot and Muse: start a fresh chat for each new job and paste only what that job needs.

How much the AI can hold in mind in one chat. Anthropic calls it the model's "working memory" and says accuracy drops as it fills up (Anthropic).

  • Why it matters: a very long chat can lose the start. In any tool, start a fresh chat for each new job.

How much the AI can keep in mind in one chat. Anthropic calls it the AI's "working memory". It gets less exact as the chat gets long (Anthropic).

Hallucination

A confident, fluent answer that is wrong: a made-up fact, price, supplier or source. Canada's guide describes AI content "that appears to be well developed, credible and reasonable" but is in fact wrong, and says it is "sometimes referred to as a 'hallucination'" (Government of Canada).

  • Why it matters: a hallucination reads exactly like a right answer. Check every name, price, date and link before it goes to a customer.
  • Example: the AI invents a parts supplier with a phone number in a draft email. The fix is a rule, not a better tool: nothing with a name or a number goes out unchecked.

When AI makes something up and says it as fact: a fake price, supplier or source. Canada's guide uses this word for content that looks credible but is wrong (Government of Canada).

  • Why it matters: a wrong answer looks just like a right one. Check names, prices, dates and links before a customer sees them.

When AI makes something up and says it like a fact. It can sound very sure (Government of Canada).

  • Why it matters: a wrong answer looks just like a right one. Check names, prices, dates and links before a customer sees them.

Grounding

Giving the AI your real facts at the moment it answers: pasting your price list into the prompt, uploading your policy to a project, or connecting it to your files. Google describes grounding as connecting "model output to verifiable data sources, reducing hallucinations" (vendor description, Google Cloud).

  • Why it matters: it is the most useful habit in this glossary. It lowers the guessing but does not end it.
  • The catch: grounding is only as good as your facts. An old price list gives an old price, said with confidence.

Giving the AI your real facts when it answers: paste in your price list, or add your files to a project. Google says this reduces made-up answers (vendor claim, Google Cloud).

  • Why it matters: it is the most useful habit here. But old facts give old answers, said with confidence.

Giving the AI your real facts when you ask. Paste in your price list. Or add your files to a project. Google says this cuts down made-up answers (Google Cloud).

  • Why it matters: it is the best habit in this list. But old facts give old answers.

Agent

AI that works out the steps of a job itself and uses tools, such as a browser, your files or another app, to do them.

  • Why it matters: the quick test is who decides the steps. If you wrote them in advance, it is automation. If the AI picks them as it goes, it is an agent.
  • Example: "Find three suppliers for this brake part and compare price and delivery" is an agent job. The agent searches, reads and builds the table.
