Question 1 of 9

What goes wrong between an order and a delivery, and what does it cost?

What goes wrong is ordinary: boxes come up short, extras show up, an item never ships, a supplier swaps in something similar, or an order arrives in two loads and only one gets written down. Each one is small. Paid without checking, they add up.

Accountants have a name for the fix. A three-way match compares three things before a bill is paid: the purchase order (what you agreed), the receiving record (what arrived) and the supplier's invoice (what they want paid). This guide builds the first half, purchase order against what arrived, because that is the part most small businesses skip. Once you have it, checking the invoice is one more column.

How often does a bill need a second look? An accounts payable benchmark by Ardent Partners puts it at 19.9% of invoices on average, against 11.8% for the best teams, at an average cost of US$9.90 to process one invoice (Silent Infotech, citing Ardent Partners' 2026 benchmark, Nov 12, 2026). Silent Infotech builds custom invoice tools, and I could not open the Ardent report itself, so treat these as a rough guide, not a law of nature. Nobody has published an independent study of how often small businesses specifically get shorted.

Your situation decides how much this matters:

  • A shop receiving stock. A few suppliers, a few orders a month. A short box costs you the item and maybe a lost sale. A monthly check is plenty.
  • A maker buying materials. Wax, fabric, jars, parts. A missing input can stop production, and a substitution can change your product. Check every delivery before you use it.
  • A distributor or a supplier selling to big retailers. Here you are checked too. Large retailers match your shipments against their purchase orders and take money off your payment when they don't agree. Your own records are your defence, so you want them matched before the retailer does it for you.

Question 2 of 9

What files do I need, and what should match?

You need two files with one row per item: your purchase orders and your receiving records. A third, the supplier invoice, comes later.

The columns that matter:

File Columns you need
Purchase orders PO number, supplier, SKU (your item code), item name, quantity ordered, unit cost, date
Receiving records Receipt or packing slip number, PO number as written, supplier, SKU, quantity received, date received, notes

Where those files come from depends on your tools. QuickBooks Online Canada includes purchase orders and inventory on the Plus (C$110 a month) and Advanced (C$220 a month) plans, not on Lite or EasyStart, with taxes not included (QuickBooks Canada pricing, checked 2026-11-27). Xero's accounting product can raise purchase orders, but it keeps no receiving record and does not compare a bill's quantities or prices with the purchase order, so the match is manual (Aequo AP, Nov 13, 2026). If you write receipts on paper, a simple spreadsheet log is enough. Type each packing slip into it the day it arrives.

Two habits make the match work. Write the PO number on every packing slip as it comes in, exactly as it appears on the order. And record what arrived, not what the order says should have arrived.

Question 3 of 9

How does the matcher work?

The matcher lines up every purchase order line with what was received for it, then sorts each line into matched, short, over, not shipped, substituted, or not on any order. It adds up the dollar value of every line that needs a look.

Here is the example. Copperleaf Candle Co. is a made-up business, a two-person candle maker that buys wax, jars, lids, wicks, fragrance oils, labels and boxes from five suppliers. In November it placed five purchase orders, 11 lines worth $3,242.00 in total. Its receiving log has 12 rows. I wrote the problems into the files on purpose, the way they show up in real life:

What happened Line What the matcher says Value to check
Short shipment Coconut wax, ordered 6, got 4 SHORT by 2 $82.00
Short shipment Mailer boxes, ordered 6, got 5 SHORT by 1 $39.00
Never shipped CD-14 wicks, ordered 2, got 0 NOT SHIPPED $54.00
Swapped item Fig oil ordered, fig and vanilla arrived SUBSTITUTED $232.00
Over shipment Black lids, ordered 15, got 17 OVER by 2 $19.20
Not ordered Square warning labels, 2 rolls NOT ON ANY PO price unknown
Split delivery Amber jars, 9 then 6 MATCH (split) $0
PO typed as "1042" and "PO 1042" three receipt rows Read as PO-1042 $0
PO number left blank two rows from the label supplier Matched by supplier and SKU $0

Six of the 11 order lines match. Five need a person, worth $426.20 of the $3,242.00 ordered, about 13%. That share is just this made-up example; yours will be different.

Three choices in the matcher matter more than the code:

  1. It matches on exact codes first. PO number plus SKU decides almost every line. Research on product matching points the same way: a simple word-overlap rule did as well as a large language model, and a tuned prompt did worse at full scale (F1 0.950 for the rule against 0.948 for the model on 2,194 product pairs, Shrestha, arXiv 2608.18158). Use AI only for the leftovers.
  2. It cleans up PO numbers but never guesses quantities. "1042" and "PO 1042" become PO-1042 because that order exists. A blank PO number is matched only when exactly one open order from that supplier has that SKU. Anything else goes on the list for a person.
  3. It shows substitutions as their own status. A swap is not a short shipment. You may be happy with fig and vanilla, or it may be the wrong scent for a product you've already labelled. Only you know.

Question 4 of 9

How do I do the match in ChatGPT, Claude or my spreadsheet?

Upload your two files, give the tool the exact columns to match on, and tell it to compute with code, not in its head. Then check its totals yourself before you act on anything.

Here is the prompt I use. Change the column names to match your files.

I'm uploading two CSV files: purchase_orders.csv and receiving_records.csv.
Use code to do every calculation. Do not estimate.
1. Count the rows in each file and tell me the counts first.
2. Clean the PO number in receiving_records (for example "1042" or "PO 1042" means PO-1042 only if that PO exists).
3. If a receiving row has no PO number, match it only when exactly one PO from that supplier has that SKU. Otherwise list it as unmatched.
4. Add up quantity received per PO number and SKU (some orders arrive in more than one delivery).
5. For every PO line, show: PO, supplier, SKU, ordered, received, difference, unit cost, status (MATCH, SHORT, OVER, NOT SHIPPED).
6. List every received SKU that is on no PO line. If its PO has a line with nothing received, call it a possible SUBSTITUTION.
7. Value each mismatch at difference x unit cost and give the total.
Give me the full list, not a summary, and a downloadable CSV of it. Read dates as year-month-day.

What to check every time, in any tool:

  1. Row counts. The tool's count of rows must equal your file's. If it says 10 receiving rows and you have 12, stop.
  2. Totals. Re-add the mismatch values yourself with SUM. In the Copperleaf files the answer is $426.20.
  3. Dates. In one independent test of invoice reading, the main mistake was reading US dates day first; one sentence telling the model to read dates in the supplier's own format lifted the cheapest model from 64% to 97% on US-style invoices (Pantalytics, Nov 13, 2026; Pantalytics sells an AI product for Odoo and published its method). That's why the prompt says "read dates as year-month-day".
  4. Nothing filled in. If a quantity is blank, the answer must say blank, not a guess.

No one has published an independent test of AI doing this exact purchase-order match. The tools are good at it when you give them clean files and make them use code. They are not good enough to skip the four checks above.

Question 6 of 9

What do I do when something doesn't match?

Write it down, tell the supplier quickly and specifically, and don't use the goods you plan to send back. The match gives you the facts. What you do with them is a business decision, and sometimes a legal one.

In the US

Under the Uniform Commercial Code, which each state adopts, you generally accept goods when you tell the seller they're fine, when you fail to reject them properly after a reasonable chance to inspect, or when you act like the owner, for example by using them (UCC 2-606, Cornell LII). If you reject, name the specific problem: a defect you don't mention can't be relied on later if the seller could have fixed it (UCC 2-605).

In Canada

Each province has its own sale of goods law. In Ontario, if a supplier delivers less than you ordered you may reject it, but if you accept it you pay the contract rate; if they deliver more, you can keep what you ordered and reject the rest (Sale of Goods Act, s. 29). You are treated as having accepted goods if you say so, act as the owner, or keep them past a reasonable time without saying you reject them (s. 34). Both sections give way to your contract and the usual practice in your trade. Quebec uses its Civil Code instead.
