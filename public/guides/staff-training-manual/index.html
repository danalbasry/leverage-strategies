Question 1

Is a written manual worth it for a small business?

Sometimes. It depends on the job.

No study I found says how much a manual helps a small business. But one survey asked about 400 oil and gas workers why they skip written steps. Half said they could not find the right one. Almost as many could not find the step they needed inside it. Almost half said it was out of date (Drilling Contractor).

For some jobs, yes. For others, a checklist, a photo or a short video works better.

How much you write also depends on how often you hire, how many people do the job, and whether an inspector or insurer could ask what you trained people on.

Never include passwords, alarm codes or customer names, even if I say them.

In the US

  • Chemicals. OSHA's Hazard Communication standard requires employers to train workers on the hazardous chemicals in their work area at their first assignment and whenever a new chemical hazard is introduced (29 CFR 1910.1200). That paragraph sets no annual refresher; other OSHA standards carry their own training rules, so check the ones for your industry rather than copying a generic "annual training" line.
  • Pay. Under federal law, time in training counts as work time unless it is outside normal hours, voluntary, not job related and no other work is done (DOL Fact Sheet #22). Reading a required manual or taking its quiz is paid time. Your state may add rules of its own.

In Canada

  • Ontario safety training. Every worker must complete basic health and safety awareness training as soon as practicable, and supervisors within one week of starting to supervise. You must keep a record and give written proof on request, including for six months after someone leaves (O. Reg. 297/13). Your manual does not replace that training.
  • Ontario pay. "Time spent in training that is required by the employer or by law is counted as work time" (Ontario ESA guide).
  • Quebec language and pay. The Charter of the French Language requires training documents produced for staff to be in French; if another language version exists, the French one must be available on terms at least as favourable (Charter s. 41). The CNESST says workers are at work when they are doing training and must be paid for those hours (CNESST).
  • Food. In Ontario, every food service premise must have at least one certified food handler or supervisor on site while it operates (Middlesex-London Health Unit). A manual explains your routine; it does not certify anyone.
  • Other provinces have their own safety acts. Check your province's worker-training rules before the manual says anything about them.

This is general information, not legal advice. Ask your regulator or a lawyer about your own situation.

  • Quiz again after a change. Re-run the quiz prompt on the new version and send it to the people the change affects.
  • Make it easy to say a step is wrong, and thank whoever says it. People who fear blame keep quiet, and the manual drifts away from the real job.
