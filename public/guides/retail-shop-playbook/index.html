Question 1 of 7

Which jobs in a shop is AI actually good at today?

AI is good today at four shop jobs: drafting product copy and signs, answering routine customer questions, answering questions about your own sales, and drafting a reorder list. It is not good at deciding on its own what to buy, setting prices, or knowing anything about your suppliers that isn't written down.

Retail is behind other industries, which is a reason to start small, not a reason to wait.

In the US

In the US. About 14% of retail businesses were using AI as of May 3, 2026, against 19.8% of all US businesses, in the Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (US Census Bureau).

In Canada

In Canada. 19.2% of Canadian businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services in the 12 months before the Q2 2026 survey, up from 12.2% a year earlier (Statistics Canada). A year earlier, only 5.7% of retail businesses planned to use AI in the next 12 months, against 14.5% of all businesses (Statistics Canada).

Question 3 of 7

How do I work out what to reorder, and when?

Reorder when what you have plus what is already on the way drops to your reorder point: the units you sell a day, times the supplier's lead time, plus a few days of safety stock. Then order enough to get you to the next order, rounded up to the supplier's case size.

The [LINK: #3] dashboard does these sums for you. What I want to show here is the one mistake that trips up most reorder sums, because it makes every tool, AI or not, order too little of your best sellers.

The stockout trap. If an item was out of stock for two weeks, it sold nothing in those two weeks. Divide its sales by all the days in the period and it looks slower than it is, so you order less of exactly the item customers wanted. Lightspeed's own forecast avoids this: it divides units sold over a 42-day lookback by the days the item was in stock, not all 42 (Lightspeed support). A chatbot won't do that unless you give it the out-of-stock days and tell it to.

Worked example (made-up shop). Harbour Street Gifts is a made-up gift shop. I gave it eight items with 42 days of sales, days out of stock, stock on hand, stock already on order, supplier lead time and case size. Then I computed the answer key in Python with these rules: daily rate counts in-stock days only, reorder point is lead time plus 7 days of safety stock, and each order covers lead time plus 28 days plus safety stock, minus on hand and on order, rounded up to a full case.

What this example can't show. It has no seasons. A 42-day rate in early November will under-order gift lines for December and over-order summer lines in spring. How do I ask ChatGPT or Claude about my sales file?

Export two files, clean them, upload them, ask a precise question, and check the answer against the file before you order anything. The checking is not optional: in GDP.xlsx, a benchmark of 70 realistic workplace spreadsheet tasks published November 13, 2026, the best of 20 model setups met every requirement on only 38.3% of attempts, and the median setup on 19.3% (Garre, Mehta and Chen, arXiv). That's an independent test by a firm that sells evaluation work, not by the AI makers. It means fewer than 4 in 10 fully right, even at the top.

Get the files ready.

  1. Export sales by item for the last 6 to 8 weeks (date, SKU, item, quantity sold, net sales), and a stock file (SKU, on hand, on order, supplier, lead time, case size).
  2. One header row, one row per record, no merged cells, numbers stored as numbers.
  3. Add a column for days out of stock, even if it's a rough count from memory. That one column fixes the stockout trap.

In ChatGPT. Make a Project for the shop so your files and standing instructions stay together. Attach the two files and ask. OpenAI's limit for CSV files and spreadsheets is "approximately 50MB, depending on the size of each row" (OpenAI Help Center). A file can upload and still not be fully analysed, so ask it to list the rows it used.

In Claude. Make a Project, add the files, and ask. Claude runs code on the file and can hand back a spreadsheet with working formulas. The limit is 30MB per file for uploads and downloads, on every plan including Free (Claude Help Center). If your export is bigger, cut it to the columns you need.

In Gemini. If your sales sheet lives in Google Sheets, Gemini can work on it in the side panel. That needs Google Workspace Business Standard or Plus; Business Starter doesn't include Gemini in Sheets (Google Workspace Help).

The prompt I use (copy it):

Use only the two attached files. For each SKU: daily rate = units sold in the last 42 days divided by the days it was in stock (42 minus days out of stock). Reorder point = daily rate times (lead time + 7). Reorder if on hand plus on order is at or below the reorder point. Order quantity = daily rate times (lead time + 28 + 7), minus on hand and on order, rounded up to a full case. Show a table with every SKU, the numbers you used and your result. List any SKU you skipped and why. Do not guess missing values; write MISSING.

Test it before you trust it. Paste the Harbour Street table above (without the last three columns) into ChatGPT or Claude with this prompt and compare its answer with the answer key. Can an agent do the reordering for me?

An agent can do most of the work around a reorder, but not the order itself. Today the agents that work best for a shop are the ones inside your till or store admin, because they see live stock without a file export. Every serious one stops before money moves: it drafts, you approve, you send and pay.

What each one does today:

  • Shopify Sidekick. Recommends what to reorder from your sales history and drafts the purchase order, "pre-filled with the products, quantities, and cost prices" (Shopify blog). It "presents changes for your review before applying them" (Shopify Help Center). Included in Shopify plans.

Question 6 of 7

What can I say on product listings and signs?

You can say anything that's true and that you can prove, and the shop owns every claim the AI writes. Three claims trip shops up most: where a product is made, "was" prices, and French in Quebec.

Where it's made.

In the US

In the US. An unqualified "Made in USA" claim needs the product to be "all or virtually all" made in the US, and flags or maps can make the claim for you. A qualified claim such as "Made in U.S. from Imported Parts" is the honest form when parts come from abroad (FTC).

In Canada

In Canada. For non-food products, "Product of Canada" needs at least 98% of total direct costs incurred in Canada and the last substantial transformation in Canada. "Made in Canada" needs at least 51%, the last substantial transformation in Canada, and a qualifying statement such as "Made in Canada with imported parts". A maple leaf or flag can count as the claim (Competition Bureau, March 7, 2025).

Worked example (made-up product). Harbour Street Gifts decorates an imported blank mug in Toronto. I added up its direct costs: blank mug from Portugal C$3.10, decal printed in Ontario C$0.45, decorating labour C$2.80, kiln firing and direct overhead C$0.40. That's C$6.75 in all, of which C$3.65 (54.1%) is Canadian. So "Product of Canada" fails. The 51% cost test for "Made in Canada with imported parts" passes, but the claim also needs the last substantial transformation in Canada, and whether decorating a finished mug counts is a judgment I wouldn't leave to an AI or make without advice. The safe line is the literal truth: "Decorated by hand in Toronto." In the US, "Made in USA" would fail outright.

"Was" prices.

In the US

In the US. A former price must be "the actual, bona fide price at which the article was offered to the public on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period of time". The FTC's own example of a false claim is raising a pen from $7.50 to $10 for a few days and then advertising "Were $10, Now Only $7.50" (16 CFR 233.1).

In Canada

In Canada. Your own "regular price" must pass one of two tests: you sold a substantial volume at that price or higher within a reasonable period, or you offered it at that price or higher in good faith for a substantial period (Competition Act, s. 74.01). Keep your price history by item so you can show it.

In Quebec. Signs, labels and websites have their own French-language rules. Have a fluent French speaker check anything an AI translates, and check the Office québécois de la langue française's current guidance for signs. In Canada. "Product of Canada" needs at least 98% of the direct costs in Canada. "Made in Canada" needs at least 51%, plus words like "with imported parts". Both need the last big step of making it to happen in Canada. A maple leaf can count as the claim (Competition Bureau).

Worked example (made-up product). Harbour Street Gifts decorates an imported mug in Toronto. Its costs are C$6.75. C$3.65 of that, about 54%, is Canadian. So "Product of Canada" fails. The 51% test passes, but whether decorating a mug counts as the last big step is a question for an adviser, not an AI. The safe words: "Decorated by hand in Toronto."
