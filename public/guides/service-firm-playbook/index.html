Question 1 of 7

Which jobs in a service firm is AI actually good at today?

AI is good at the office around the work: first drafts of quotes, proposals and emails, summaries of calls and long threads, and reminders. It does not clean the office, cut the hair or sign the tax return, and the best evidence says it should not decide anything on its own yet.

Use is real but shallow. In the US, about 1 in 5 businesses used AI in a business function in the two weeks before each Census survey from December 2025 to May 2026, and fewer than 1 in 5 firms with four or fewer employees did (US Census Bureau). The Federal Reserve's 2025 small business survey found 46% of employer firms said their business or staff used AI tools, but only 31% of all respondents reported more sales from their firm's use of AI (New York Fed).

In Canada

19.2% of businesses used AI to produce goods or deliver services in the year to spring 2026, up from 6.1% two years earlier. Professional, scientific and technical services were at 32.4%, and 40% of all businesses said AI was not relevant to them (Statistics Canada). In CFIB's August 2026 survey, 21% of small firms had adopted AI and 33% were exploring; personal services had the lowest engagement of any sector, with 42% having adopted or exploring AI (CFIB).

Question 3 of 7

How do I turn call notes into a quote without a wrong price?

Give the AI three things: the client's notes, your rate card, and one past quote with names removed. Tell it to use only the rate card, to flag anything that is not on it, and to show where every number came from. Then you check every number before it goes out.

The risk is not a wild made-up figure. In a published test of ChatGPT, Gemini and NotebookLM on a 300-document set, 30% of answers had at least one error, and most were "interpretive overconfidence", not invented numbers (Hagar et al., arXiv). In a quote, that looks like a service slipped in, a discount assumed or a minimum forgotten, all written in a confident voice.

Worked example (made up). Brightline Office Cleaning Example business is an example business I invented for this guide, with three client calls and one rate card. I built the answer key in code so you can grade any tool's draft against it.

The prompt I use (copy it, fill the brackets):

Prompt
Draft a quote for [CLIENT NAME] using ONLY the attached rate card and call notes. Follow the format of the attached past quote. Rules: do not change any price. If something the client asked for is not on the rate card, write [PRICE TO CONFIRM] and list it at the end. Do not add discounts, dates or guarantees that are not in the notes. Show tax as its own line. End with a table of every line, its price and where it came from (rate card or notes).

Then send a second message: "Check the draft against the rate card line by line and list any price that does not match exactly."

Question 4 of 7

Can I record and summarize client calls?

Yes, if you announce it every time. That one habit covers most of the rules on both sides of the border, and it is what Canada's Privacy Commissioner asks businesses to do.

In the US

Federal law lets a party to a call record with one party's consent, but some states require everyone on the call to agree, and a call that crosses state lines may fall under the stricter rule. So announce on every call. [DAN: CONFIRM THE LIST OF ALL-PARTY CONSENT STATES ON THE REPORTERS COMMITTEE (RCFP) GUIDE OR STATE PAGES BEFORE NAMING ANY STATES.]

In Canada

The Criminal Code lets you record a call you are part of, but PIPEDA governs what a business does. The Privacy Commissioner's guidance, updated 8 Oct 2026, says to tell people the call is recorded, state a narrow purpose, offer a way to continue without recording, keep recordings only as long as needed, and get express consent where voiceprints are involved (OPC). Quebec's private-sector privacy law adds a privacy impact assessment before personal information leaves Quebec.

The script I would use: "We're recording and transcribing this call with [TOOL] so we have an accurate record of decisions and next steps. If you'd rather we didn't, we can carry on without it."

Built-in notes are usually the cheapest start: Gemini in Meet comes with Google Workspace Business Standard and up (Google). Turn off auto-join for every meeting, and read the summary before you send action items to a client.

Question 5 of 7

What should never go into an AI tool?

Some things never go in, on any plan: passwords and login codes, full government ID numbers, card and bank numbers, privileged legal material, and another client's unredacted file. Everything else with client details goes only into your firm's approved business plan.

The trap is the personal account. Anthropic's consumer terms for Free, Pro and Max let you choose whether chats train its models, with five-year retention if you opt in; Team and Enterprise are not covered by that change (Anthropic). OpenAI says it does not train on ChatGPT Business data by default (OpenAI), and Microsoft says Copilot prompts and responses are not used to train the underlying models (Microsoft Canada). None of that protects a staff member pasting client files into a personal account.

In Canada

The Privacy Commissioner's generative AI principles say to use anonymized, synthetic or de-identified data instead of personal information where you can, and treat AI outputs about an identifiable person as collecting personal information (OPC).

If you are in a regulated profession, your regulator's rules come first. Lawyers, accountants, insurance brokers and real estate licensees each have guidance on confidentiality and checking AI output; ask your professional body for its current guidance before client data goes in.

Question 6 of 7

Where does an agent fit, and where do I approve?

An agent can take a multi-step job, like "turn today's call notes into a draft quote and file it", and run it while you do something else. Keep it on drafts and filing. Sending, booking, charging and anything a client sees waits for you.

  • ChatGPT Work (launched 9 Jul 2026) is the agent inside ChatGPT. OpenAI says "You decide what it can access, when it should check in, and when it needs your approval before taking action" (OpenAI).
  • Claude Cowork runs on paid Claude plans with three approval modes: Manual, Auto and Skip. Anthropic's own advice: for "money, messages sent as you, important files", stay close and consider Manual (Anthropic). Claude in Chrome says "Purchases, financial actions, and anything you can't undo wait for you" (Anthropic).
  • Muse, Meta's agent, is what I use every day for research and errands. Its small business version connects to tools like QuickBooks, Stripe, Shopify and Zoom, and is free with usage limits (TechCrunch). I keep client files out of it.
  • Your accounting software may already have one. QuickBooks Canada's Plus and Advanced plans "Draft invoices for quick review through simple chat, and send prioritized payment reminders for unpaid items on approval" (beta) (QuickBooks Canada). That "on approval" is the model to copy everywhere.

A workflow across tools (example): Muse gathers the facts for a proposal. Claude or ChatGPT drafts it from your rate card. A Zapier step called Human in the Loop pauses and asks you to approve, edit or decline before anything moves on (Zapier). You send it from your own email. Zapier Professional starts from US$19.99 a month, and on that plan approval requests can only go to you (Zapier pricing).

In Canada

Before an automated "we miss you, book again" message goes out, check CASL. It needs consent, identification and an unsubscribe option, and it covers texts as well as email (CRTC). AI can write the message; it cannot create the consent.
